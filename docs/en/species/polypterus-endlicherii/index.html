1. Introduction — how this page was made

Polypterus endlicherii is a large member of the bichirs (Polypterus), the ancient fishes of Africa, whose lower jaw sticks out beyond the upper jaw. A Japanese article introducing rare tropical fish puts it first of its 10 species, and writes, “Not many are traded, and they are not often handled except at fairly large aquarium shops.” (translated) (［17］).

On the other hand, another article on the same site introduces this fish as “the most widely traded species among the bichirs” (translated) (［18］). This page checks which of the two matches the records, going back to the original description of 1847. It also checks what the names often seen in shops,「エンドリケリー・エンドリケリー」(endlicherii endlicherii) and「コンゴエンドリケリー」(Congo endlicherii), refer to.

This page was made by collecting and cross-checking the following kinds of information.

  • The original description, a catalog of names, and databases: the 1847 original description (［1］), a catalog of fish scientific names (［2］), a 2019 revision of the family Polypteridae (［3］; the main text was not opened), FishBase (［4］［5］), a database of the origins of scientific names (［6］), a paper on a related species in Indonesia (［7］), and a US database of nonindigenous aquatic species (［8］)
  • Articles, databases and forums from abroad: pages of a German ornamental fish importer (［9］［10］), a keeping database (［11］), an article in a British magazine (［12］), a breeding article in a US magazine and its repost on a forum (［13］), and the English Wikipedia (［14］)
  • Keeping records from Japan: keepers’ blogs (［15］［16］)
  • Articles, shop pages and price information: articles (［17］〜［19］), a shop’s blog and a shop page (［20］［21］), and auction results (［22］)
  • Records of regulations: the CITES species database (［23］), and pages of the Ministry of the Environment, Japanese law, and the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (［24］〜［28］)

There are 4 rules for reading this page (the meanings of terms are also collected in the glossary).

  1. Numbers have sources. A ［number］ corresponds to the reference list at the end; clicking it takes you to that reference.
  2. "(needs checking)" marks things such as hearsay or a shop’s introduction, which may have been misheard or miswritten.
  3. "(unverified)" marks things whose original source could not be opened and which could not be confirmed. The most detailed breeding record (1994; ［13］) is marked “(unverified)”, because the original magazine article could not be opened and the text was read as reposted on a forum.
  4. “Data from a related species” means numbers for fish other than Polypterus endlicherii (such as P. senegalus). The author’s interpretations are marked “(inference)”.

And what is not known is written down as not known. No record that measured the water temperature or water chemistry of the habitat was found. No record of how it spawns in the wild has been found either. The open questions are collected in “7. What is still unknown”.

2. Identity

The original description of 1847

This fish was first described by the Austrian ichthyologist J. J. Heckel. The description appears in the volume on the fishes of the Nile in the travel account of J. von Russegger (［1］［2］). The original is in German and Latin, and the translations and summaries below are by the author (AI).

  • It says that the museum in Vienna had a bichir from the White Nile a little over 2 Schuh (a unit of length of the time) long, and that, since it differed in important points from P. bichir, the only species known from the Nile until then, it should be regarded as a new species (［1］). The fraction of the length could not be read, because the numbers were garbled in the text read (text read by machine), so it is unverified.
  • About the name, it says that the botanist Professor Endlicher happened to come by just as this new species was being examined, and allowed his name to be given to it (［1］).
  • As differences from P. bichir, it gives the head and the front of the body being flattened as if pressed from above, the forehead being broad and hollowed, the bony spines on the back (the bones of the finlets) being broad and flat, the lateral line being interrupted twice, and the surface of the scales looking wrinkled (［1］).
  • The fin counts are given as “P.42. V.14. D.12. A.13. C.20.”, and the scale rows as 55 (［1］).
  • About the color, it says that the only specimen at hand was 1 dried fish from Khartoum (today the capital of Sudan), much darker than P. bichir, with 5 broad, dark bands running from the back down to half the height of the body (［1］).

“Endlicherii endlicherii” and “congicus”

The name「ポリプテルス・エンドリケリー・エンドリケリー」(Polypterus endlicherii endlicherii), often seen in shops (［19］［21］), is what this species was called when it was divided into 2 subspecies.

  • Congo endlicherii: in the trade in Japan, congicus is sometimes called「コンゴエンドリケリー」(Congo endlicherii),「エンドリケリー・コンギクス」(endlicherii congicus) or「ビチャー」(bichir) (［19］［22］). Fish from Lake Tanganyika were sold as「タンガニーカコンギクス」(Tanganyika congicus) and「ギセラビチャー」(Gisela bichir) (［22］). FishBase says that congicus is widely distributed in the Congo River basin and also occurs in Lake Tanganyika (［5］).
  • Congicus grows larger than endlicherii; FishBase’s maximum is 97.0 cm total length (［5］).

The main text of the 2019 revision (［3］) has not been opened, so the reasons for treating congicus as a separate species are unverified. A Japanese article writes that there are differences in genes and skeleton, but gives no original record (［19］; needs checking).

Origin of the name

  • The genus name Polypterus means “many fins” in Greek (［4］［9］).
  • The species name honors the Austrian botanist Stephan Endlicher (1804–1849) (［4］［6］).
  • The database of the origins of scientific names writes that Endlicher “apparently discovered” this species in the fish collection of the museum in Vienna (［6］). The English Wikipedia also writes that Endlicher was the first to identify this fish (［14］). However, the text of the original description is written as Endlicher happening to come by while it was being examined and allowing his name to be used, and does not say who found it (［1］).

How to tell it from similar species

  • Among the bichirs there is a group called「下顎系」(the “lower-jaw type”), whose lower jaw sticks out beyond the upper jaw; endlicherii, congicus and P. bichir belong to it (［11］［18］［19］).
  • As features of endlicherii, FishBase gives the lower jaw being longer than the upper jaw, the pectoral fin reaching the first spine on the back, 11–14 small fins on the back (finlets), a short distance to the start of the dorsal fin of 21.7–27.4% of the standard length, and 4–5 (rarely 6) broad, dark bands (［4］).
  • Congicus has 12–15 finlets and 6–9 dark bands (［5］). A Japanese article gives P. bichir 14–18 finlets (［19］).
  • A Japanese article writes that fish called「ラプエン」(lap-en), hybrids of endlicherii and P. bichir lapradei, are occasionally seen in the wild too, so telling them apart by pattern has become difficult (［19］). No original record is given, so this needs checking.

Where it lives and how

  • FishBase says it is a freshwater fish living near the bottom, in swamps and rivers. It says it feeds mainly on fish, and also eats snails and crustaceans (［4］).
  • It is said to be a fish that can breathe air but can live without doing so (air breathing is supplementary) (［4］).
  • The keeping database writes that it lives in areas with many rivers and swamps, and is sometimes found in brackish water (water mixed with seawater) around mangroves. It also says that it is active at night, has poor eyesight, and finds food by smell (［11］).

Water temperature and chemistry — no measurements found

No record that measured the water temperature, pH or electrical conductivity of the habitat was found in the sources opened this time. What the keeping guidelines (chapter 5) are based on is not known either.

Origin labels of fish coming into Japan

At auctions in 2026, wild fish were labeled with origins such as the following (［22］).

  • Guinea (many labeled “DABOLA” or “FARANAH”)
  • Nigeria (some labeled「YAWA川」(Yawa River))
  • Ghana (「ホワイトボルタ川」(White Volta River),「タイン川」(Tain River))

Dabola and Faranah are names of towns in Guinea, and are thought to be in the upper Niger basin (inference; whether the label is where the fish were caught or where they were gathered is not known). A Japanese shop’s blog writes that the Guinea fish that came in long ago and the Guinea fish of recent years come from different collecting sites, and that fish with thick bands like those of the past have not come in for the last few years (［20］; needs checking).

The most detailed record is the 1994 record from an aquarium

  • An article in a British magazine says that, of the bichirs that have spawned successfully in captivity, endlicherii was the 3rd species, after P. senegalus (1964) and P. ornatipinnis (1966 and others), with the success by Azuma in 1995 (［12］).
  • That 1995 article appeared in the US magazine TFH (［12］). The author could not open the original magazine, and read the text as reposted on a US forum in 2008 (［13］). The reposted text says that full success did not come until last summer, and the 2nd spawning was in August 1994, which matches the record that the article came out in 1995 (［12］［13］).
  • The author of the article writes that he bought the fish from a Japanese importer, so he appears to be a Japanese hobbyist (inference) (［13］).

The 1st and 2nd rows of the table above are this record. In the 1st spawning in 1992, several hundred eggs were laid, but none were fertilized. In the 2nd spawning 2 years later, they spawned in a tank to which a floating island and waterside plants had been added and in which filtration and aeration had been strengthened, and about 350 fry hatched (［13］).

Spawning in Japan in 2008 (unfertilized eggs)

A Japanese keeper’s blog records that 2-year-old females spawned (［15］). The water temperature was kept low at 24.5 °C, and from late November they had almost stopped eating, but when at the end of January a large water change was made and the temperature raised to 27 °C, the females swam around violently, and the next day there were eggs all over the bottom of the tank (［15］). The only fish kept with them was a male P. bichir lapradei, and the keeper writes that they were probably unfertilized eggs (［15］).

The amount of water changed is written「4/3ほど」(“about 4/3”) in the text, which may be a slip for three quarters (inference). The「2/1」(“2/1”) in the same post may also be a slip for one half (inference).

Farming and strains in Japan

  • The German importer writes that in March 2016 it became able to stock fish bred in Indonesia regularly, and that they had become much cheaper than wild fish (［9］). It also writes that it received bred young in July 2019 (［10］).
  • A Japanese article writes that the fish traded in Japan are mainly fish bred in Southeast Asia (［18］).
  • A Japanese shop’s blog introduces a domestic strain named after a hobbyist, with young obtained in June 2009 and in 2020 from wild parents from Guinea (［20］). It is a shop’s introduction and does not describe the breeding itself, so it needs checking. At auction too, there was a fish labeled「ブリード」(“bred”) with that name (［22］).
  • No farming record for this species describing how it is bred (for example, whether hormones are used) was found.

5. Keeping and breeding

The short answer

Lining up the records, the keys to keeping it are as follows (inference).

  1. Provide a large tank (growth often stops at 40–50 cm, but once it reaches 50 cm, a tank 120 cm wide × 60 cm deep or more［18］)
  2. Fit the lid firmly (there is a record of a glass lid being broken before spawning［15］)
  3. Do not let the water get dirty (it eats a lot, and its droppings easily foul the water［19］)
  4. Do not keep it with fish small enough to fit in its mouth (［11］［19］)

Size and growth

  • FishBase’s maximum is 65.5 cm standard length and 3.4 kg (［4］). Some sources give a maximum of 70 cm (［9］［19］) or 75 cm (［11］). The author of the 1994 record writes that it reaches about 63 cm (25 inches) (［13］).
  • Japanese articles write that in captivity growth often stops at around 40–50 cm, and that the average is around 40 cm (［18］［19］).
  • In the 1994 record, the fish were 9.5–10.5 cm at 70 days, 13–15 cm at 180 days and 15–20 cm at 300 days (［13］; unverified).
  • On a Japanese blog, a fish that was about 7 cm in January 2020 (with its external gills still present) had grown to about 18 cm by February 2022, and its external gills were gone. It was fed about 2 pellets of prepared food a day (［16］). The post says, “I started keeping it just about a year ago” (translated), but the post about starting to keep it is dated 2 years earlier, so the posts do not agree (［16］).

Food — sources say different things

  • The keeping database says that it does not usually eat dry food, but that some fish have eaten pellets (［11］).
  • The author of the 1994 record writes that all bichirs prefer live fish, but that they are very adaptable and usually eat dry food too (［13］).
  • The fish on Japanese blogs ate prepared food (［15］［16］), and a Japanese article also says that they get used to prepared food (［19］).
  • The conflict between many fish bred in Southeast Asia being traded (［18］) and the statement that “it does not eat dry food” (［11］) may be due to the difference between wild and bred fish (inference).

Tankmates and temperament

  • The keeping database says that it cannot be kept with fish small enough to fit in its mouth, but is otherwise relatively peaceful (［11］). A Japanese article also says that, among large fish, it can be kept with many others (［19］).
  • A Japanese article says that wild fish are somewhat more aggressive than bred fish (［18］).
  • The fish on a Japanese blog had its tail fin bitten and damaged by other fish, but it grew back neatly (［16］).

Lifespan

A Japanese article says that the average is 10–15 years, and that it sometimes lives 20 years or more (［18］). The English Wikipedia says about 20 years in the wild and over 20 years in captivity, but gives no source (［14］).

Breeding

Thinking from the records above (chapter 4), the clues for breeding are as follows (inference).

  • Have a mature male and female (in the 1st spawning, the male may not have fertilized the eggs because he was young［13］).
  • There is a record of spawning at 28–29 °C in soft, slightly acidic water (［13］).
  • Move the eggs to a hatching tank right away (［13］［19］).
  • Raise the fry separately, with plenty of hiding places (［13］［19］).

6. Before you buy

Is it really a rare fish?

Lining up the records, endlicherii as a species cannot be called a rare fish in Japan today (inference).

  • As of March 2016, fish bred in Indonesia were coming in regularly and had become much cheaper than wild fish (［9］). A Japanese article also says that the fish traded in Japan are mainly fish bred in Southeast Asia (［18］).
  • Among winning bids on Yahoo! Auctions from May 23 to November 18, 2026 (the last 180 days), a search for「エンドリケリー ワイルド」(endlicherii wild) gave 57 results, and wild fish labeled as from Guinea, Nigeria or Ghana were sold almost every month (［22］).
  • Fish of 8–18 cm sold for 880–3,520 yen each (［22］).

On the other hand, the following seem to be few and expensive.

  • Fish with unusual colors or shapes: a Japanese article lists platinum (whitish color), short body, long fin (long fins) and devil (no eyes), and writes that short body, long fin and devil are rare and little traded (［19］). At auction, fish labeled platinum sold for 35,500–120,000 yen each (［22］).
  • Congicus from Lake Tanganyika: although it is a separate species (chapter 2), fish of 30–80 cm sold for 14,500–185,000 yen each (［22］).

The author of the 1994 record writes that the 7 fish he bought in 1986 reached him from an African collector through a German wholesaler and a Japanese importer (［13］). At that time it was probably a fish that came in only as wild fish, by a roundabout route (inference).

Wild and bred fish

Japanese articles write that bred fish have rounder faces and darker patterns, while wild fish have longer faces and sturdier bodies (［18］［19］). They also write that imported wild fish almost always carry a parasite (Macrogyrodactylus polypteri) (［19］; no original record is given, so it needs checking).

Telling the sexes apart

  • The male’s anal fin is said to be larger and thicker than the female’s (［11］［13］［19］).
  • A Japanese article writes that the male’s anal fin is a thick, rounded fan shape, while the female’s is long and narrow, and that young fish cannot be told apart (［19］).

Legality and regulation

This species is not listed by CITES (the Washington Convention), and, as far as was checked this time, it is not among the species whose import or keeping is regulated by Japanese law either. However, it is not known in which country, or under which permits, each wild fish was collected and exported, so this page cannot state whether the trade in wild fish was legal. What is known and what is not known are written separately below.

What is known

  • CITES: searching the species database of the CITES Secretariat and others (Species+) for Polypterus (genus) and Polypteridae (family) gave no records (checked November 18, 2026) (［23］). The Australian lungfish (Neoceratodus forsteri), searched for comparison, did come up (［23］). FishBase also shows “Not Evaluated” (［4］).
  • IUCN Red List: FishBase shows “Least Concern (LC)”, assessed on June 7, 2019 (［4］). Congicus is also shown as “Least Concern (LC)” (［2］［5］).
  • Origin labels of wild fish coming into Japan: at auctions in 2026 they were labeled as from Guinea, Nigeria or Ghana (［22］).
  • Bred fish: as of March 2016, fish bred in Indonesia had come to be traded regularly (［9］). There are also fish labeled as bred in Japan (［20］［21］).

What is not known

  • What permits the countries of origin, such as Guinea, Nigeria and Ghana, require for collecting and exporting freshwater ornamental fish has not been checked (unverified).
  • From which country, and with what documents, the fish at auctions and shops were imported is not known, because it is not labeled (［21］［22］). What backs up labels such as「ギニア便直輸入」(direct import on a Guinea shipment) is not known either.
  • How the farming and export of this species are regulated in Indonesia has not been checked (unverified). For the related species P. senegalus, there is a paper saying that there are no rules on its farming in Indonesia (［7］; data from a related species).
  • The IUCN Red List page itself could not be opened (access was denied), so the content of the assessment (descriptions of threats and trade) is unverified.
  • So it is not known whether the wild fish in the trade were legally collected and exported.

Japanese regulations: date checked

Checked on 2026-11-18.

  • Invasive Alien Species Act: the Ministry of the Environment’s “List of Invasive Alien Species, etc.” (showing 166 kinds of Invasive Alien Species and 52 kinds of Uncategorized Alien Species) was checked. The only fish on it are members of the orders Lepisosteiformes (gars), Cypriniformes, Siluriformes, Esociformes (pikes), Cyprinodontiformes and Perciformes; bichirs were not listed as an Invasive Alien Species, an Uncategorized Alien Species, or an organism that requires a species identification certificate (［24］).
  • List of Alien Species That May Damage Ecosystems (not a legal regulation, but a list to call for caution): bichirs were not in the file listing fish (［25］).
  • Act on Welfare and Management of Animals (Specified Animals): on the Ministry of the Environment’s page, the Specified Animals are about 650 species of mammals, birds and reptiles, such as tigers, bears, crocodiles and mamushi (a pit viper), and fish are not covered (［26］).
  • Act on Conservation of Endangered Species: in the text of the enforcement order (e-Gov law search; the version in force from March 5, 2026), the name of bichirs was not found (［27］).
  • Import permits under the Fisheries Resources Protection Act: on the page of the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (updated June 9, 2026), the fish that need an import permit are salmonids, common carp, goldfish and other Carassius fishes, bighead carp, silver carp, black carp, grass carp, Nile tilapia and red sea bream; bichirs were not included (［28］).
  • Washington Convention (CITES): as above, this species is not listed (［23］).
  • Whether this species is covered by other rules (such as prefectural ordinances) has not been checked yet (unverified).

For reference, groups of the related species P. senegalus that appear to have gone wild have been found in rivers in Indonesia (［7］; data from a related species). Not being covered by Japanese regulations does not mean that it is all right to release it (inference).

This page’s position

This page does not state whether the wild fish in the trade were legally collected and exported. As above, it sets out what is known and what is not known separately. Not being regulated under Japanese law does not mean that collecting and export in the country of origin were legal.

7. What is still unknown

Below are the gaps that the sources collected this time could not fill, and the points marked “(unverified)” or “(needs checking)” in the text.

  1. The original of the 1994 breeding record: the 1995 TFH article (volume 44, pages 116–128, as given by［12］) could not be opened, and it was read in a 2008 forum repost (［13］). Whether the repost is the same as the original is unverified. Whether the author of the article is a Japanese hobbyist is also inference.
  2. The content of the 2019 revision: the reasons for treating congicus as a separate species and for including lapradei in P. bichir are unverified, because the main text of the paper (［3］) has not been opened.
  3. The length of the specimen in the original description: the fraction in “a little over 2 Schuh” cannot be read (［1］; unverified).
  4. Water temperature and chemistry of the habitat: no measurements have been found. What the keeping guidelines (［11］［13］［18］) are based on is not known either.
  5. Spawning in the wild: no record from the wild describing when, where and how it spawns has been found.
  6. Maximum size: the sources differ, with 65.5 cm (standard length; ［4］), about 63 cm (［13］), 70 cm (［9］［19］) and 75 cm (［11］), and many do not say how it was measured. The reference behind FishBase’s figure (Ref. 123661) has not been opened.
  7. Days to hatching: the sources differ, with 58–70 hours (［13］), 3–4 days (［11］) and 3–7 days (［19］).
  8. Food: the sources differ on whether it eats dry food (［11］ versus ［13］［15］［16］［19］).
  9. Hybrids: the statement that “lap-en” are also seen in the wild (［19］) needs checking.
  10. Strains in Japan: how the domestic strain named after a hobbyist is bred comes only from a shop’s introduction and needs checking (［20］). The difference between the Guinea fish of the past and of today also needs checking (［20］).
  11. Parasites: the statement that imported wild fish almost always carry parasites (［19］) needs checking.
  12. Going wild in the US: the English Wikipedia writes that released fish formed small groups in places such as Florida in the US (［14］), but the US database of nonindigenous aquatic species has records of only P. delhezi in the genus Polypterus, and none of this species (［8］). Which is correct is not known.
  13. Lifespan: an average of 10–15 years, 20 years or more (［18］), and about 20 years in the wild (［14］; no source). No long-term keeping record by a keeper has been found.
  14. Rules in the countries of origin and Indonesia: the rules on collecting, export and farming are unverified.
  15. Japanese regulations: prefectural ordinances are unverified.
  16. Sources that could not be opened: the IUCN Red List page itself (403), the main text of the 2019 revision, a 2022 paper on food preferences and a 2024 paper on temperature preferences published in the Journal of Ichthyology (given by the English Wikipedia; neither the main text nor the abstract could be opened, so they are not used), and FishBase’s country pages and reference list (access was blocked).

If you keep endlicherii and record the origin and “bred” labels, its size when you started keeping it and now, the water temperature and chemistry, its food, and how it behaved when it spawned, that will be very useful as a record that can be compared with others.
