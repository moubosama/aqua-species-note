1. Introduction — how this page was made

The Diamond polka dot stingray (“Diamond polka” for short) is a South American freshwater stingray with white polka dots scattered over a black body (［14］［17］). One Japanese article writes that its price stands out even among freshwater stingrays: “Even cheap ones cost several hundred thousand yen, and expensive ones as much as several million yen.” (translated) (［16］).

However, on looking into it, “Diamond polka” is not the name of a species. It is a trade name given to individuals and bloodlines selected for things such as how their white spots are arranged, from among Potamotrygon leopoldi (the polka dot stingray), a freshwater stingray living in the Xingu River in Brazil (［3］［14］). This page checks against the records what “Diamond” refers to, and what is known about the original species.

This page was made by collecting and cross-checking the following kinds of information.

  • Papers and official documents: the 1970 describing paper (［1］; its main text was not opened, and the bibliographic details were checked in［2］), a catalog of fish scientific names (［2］), the proposal the Brazilian government submitted to a meeting of the Washington Convention (［3］), a survey of the breeding of wild fish in the Xingu River (［4］), and FishBase (［5］)
  • Data sheets: Seriously Fish (［6］)
  • Articles from abroad: articles in a UK fishkeeping magazine (［7］［8］)
  • Records from Japan: an aquarium’s report (［9］), tropical fish shops’ blogs (［10］［18］［19］), hobbyists’ blogs (［11］〜［13］), articles and shop pages (［14］〜［17］), and auction results (［20］)
  • Records of regulations: the CITES species database (［21］), Brazil’s rules and list of threatened species (［22］〜［25］), and pages of Japan Customs, the Ministry of the Environment and Japanese law (［26］〜［29］)

There are 4 rules for reading this page (the meanings of terms are also collected in the glossary).

  1. Numbers have sources. A ［number］ corresponds to the reference list at the end; clicking it takes you to that reference.
  2. "(needs checking)" marks things such as information picked up from auto-captions or hearsay, which may have been misheard or miswritten.
  3. "(unverified)" marks things whose original source could not be opened and which could not be confirmed.
  4. “Data from a related species” means numbers for fish other than Potamotrygon leopoldi. The author’s interpretations are marked “(inference)”.

And what is not known is written down as not known. No official definition that sets the standard for calling a fish “Diamond” was found. No record that measured the water temperature or water chemistry of the habitat was found in the sources opened this time either. The open questions are collected in “7. What is still unknown”.

First, something important. The tail has a venomous spine (［6］［15］［17］). For details, read “5. Keeping and breeding”.

2. Identity

“Diamond” is not a species but a trade name given to a pattern

To give the conclusion first: the Diamond polka dot stingray is a trade name given to pattern types and bloodlines within a single species, Potamotrygon leopoldi. No “Diamond” described as a new species or subspecies appears either in the catalog of fish scientific names (［2］) or in the CITES species database (［21］).

The sources say the following about this name.

In summary, “Diamond” is used in the following senses (inference).

  1. Originally, a product name for wild fish of a type with many white spots, caught around São Félix do Xingu on the upper Xingu River (［3］［10］［14］)
  2. Now, in Japan, the name of tank-bred bloodlines whose parents were of that type (［10］［11］［19］)

The same shop’s website also writes that abroad, the Diamond polka seems to be called “Black Diamond” (［14］). On that website, the division by “spots lined up along the edge of the disc” is written as an explanation of the small spot, and whether the same standard is also used for the Diamond polka is not clear.

The original species — Potamotrygon leopoldi

  • Description: described in 1970 by M. N. Castex and H. P. Castello (［1］［2］). The main text of the describing paper was not opened, so it is unverified.
  • The specimen the name is based on (holotype): there is only 1, kept at the IRSNB in Belgium as number 475 (［2］).
  • Type locality: a small stream on the right bank of the upper Xingu River, in the state of Mato Grosso (［2］).
  • Status of the scientific name: treated as a valid name in the catalog of fish scientific names, with no synonyms listed (［2］［21］).
  • Origin of the name: the origin of the species name was not given in the sources opened this time. Since its Chinese name in the CITES species database is「利奥波德三世江魟」(Leopold III river stingray), it may be named after King Leopold III of Belgium (inference; unverified) (［21］).
  • English names: Polka Dot Stingray (［6］), Xingu river ray (［21］), White-Blotched River Stingray (［9］). Seriously Fish also lists the German-style numbers P13/14 and P62 (［6］).

Appearance, and how it differs from similar species

  • The back is dark grey to black, with white or pale ochre spots and letter-like markings. The belly is white, and the tail also has small spots (［3］).
  • The tail has at least 2 rows of nearly parallel spines, as well as a spine for defense (［3］).
  • A similar species is P. henlei of the Tocantins–Araguaia rivers. They are said to be told apart by more numerous, smaller teeth and more tail spines (［3］). Seriously Fish writes that P. henlei has spots on the underside, and that P. leopoldi should not (［6］).
  • It is also easily confused with P. albimaculata and P. jabuti from the Tapajós River, and the proposal writes that export of these 2 species from Brazil is prohibited (［3］).
  • A rare type whose spots form rings is called “Eclipse” (［6］).

Pure-blood and hybrids

Where the Diamond polka is sold, the word “pure-blood” is often used (［10］［15］［20］). However, no record of genetic testing for whether a fish is pure-blood was found in the sources opened this time.

  • Seriously Fish writes that many members of the genus Potamotrygon hybridize easily (［6］).
  • The Brazilian proposal writes that Asian breeders began crossing them around 2000, and that by 2020, through 20 years of crossbreeding, they were producing fish of many different patterns and colors (［3］; a statement citing other sources).
  • A Japanese article also writes that hybrids with motoro, histrix and others are sold, and that pure-blood fish with nothing mixed in are very expensive (［15］).
  • A Japanese hobbyist writes that a female who looked pure-blood kept producing young that seemed to be hybrids, so they think that female is a hybrid (［11］).

In other words, it is hard to decide from appearance alone whether a fish is pure-blood (inference). Whether it is pure-blood can only be judged from records of where the parents came from.

3. Habitat

Only in the Xingu River system

P. leopoldi is endemic, living only in the Xingu River system in Brazil (［2］［3］). According to the Brazilian proposal, it lives in the Xingu River system on the lower side of the Amazon basin, including the Iriri and Curuá rivers in the state of Pará and the main tributaries of the Xingu River in the state of Mato Grosso (［3］). FishBase and Seriously Fish give the Xingu and Fresco rivers (［5］［6］).

Where it lives

The Brazilian proposalPrefers rocky, pebbly and sandy bottoms down to 3.0 m deep. Because of this, it is distributed patchily in the river. It was common at a place near Altamira called “Cotovelo”［3］
Seriously FishLike other members of the genus: sandbanks, shallows of large rivers, and slow-flowing tributaries with muddy or sandy bottoms. In the rainy season it enters flooded forest, and it is also found in lakes and pools after the water recedes (as a description of the genus)［6］

The proposal writes that newborn young, young of the year and females close to giving birth are found in the same places, and that young fish and mature males are found together (［3］).

Water temperature and chemistry — no measurements found

No record that measured the water temperature, pH or electrical conductivity of the habitat was found in the sources opened this time. FishBase gives a water temperature of 20–25 °C and pH 6.0–6.8 (［5］), but what this is based on has not been checked. The Brazilian proposal writes that the water temperature of the Xingu River has risen by at least 2 °C over the last 20 years (［3］; as unpublished data).

Changes to the habitat

The Brazilian proposal writes the following (［3］).

  • Part of an important habitat downstream of Altamira was flooded by the construction of the Belo Monte hydroelectric plant from 2011, and the habitat was lost.
  • Ten years after the dam was completed, the minimum size at which females mature had become smaller.
  • In the middle and upper Xingu River, pollution such as mercury from mining has been recorded.
  • From 2006, because of the dam construction, collecting for the aquarium trade moved upstream to São Félix do Xingu. As of 2021, most fish sold as ornamental fish come from this area (as researchers’ observations).

However, a figure counting how much the numbers have declined was not found in the sources opened this time.

What the records show when lined up

  • 1–9 young per birth: 4 at the Japanese aquarium (［9］), 4 with the Japanese keeper (［10］), 2 and 3 in the UK (［7］［8］), and 1–9 with the Japanese hobbyist (［11］). These fall within the wild range of 1–11 (［3］).
  • Numbers vary even for the same keeper: in the Japanese hobbyist’s record, a birth of only 1 came just before a birth of 9. The hobbyist writes that the number differs quite a lot depending on the mother (［11］).
  • Interval between births: at the UK shop, the 2nd birth came 10 weeks after the 1st (［8］). The Japanese hobbyist writes that the same female gives birth at intervals of about 3 months, or about once every 4 months (［11］). These intervals are shorter than the wild gestation of 4 months (［3］), so gestation may be shorter in captivity (inference). Seriously Fish writes that gestation in captivity is usually 9–12 weeks, and seems to be shorter than in the wild, perhaps because there is more food (［6］).
  • No record with water temperature figures: none of the 5 records gives figures for the water temperature or chemistry at the time of birth. The UK shop describes it only as warm, soft water low in nitrate (［7］).

Mating is rough

  • The Japanese aquarium writes that the male chases the female and bites her tail and disc, so the female becomes covered in wounds (［9］).
  • At the UK shop, the pair was separated because the male was rough. When they were left together after the birth, the female injured the male with her tail spine (［7］［8］). In the UK hobbyist’s record, the male was stung by the female’s spine and died (［8］; needs checking).
  • Seriously Fish also recommends choosing a pair in which the female is larger than the male, and having a setup ready so they can be separated (［6］).

The newborn young

  • The newborn young carry a small yolk sac (the sac of nutrients on the belly) and live on it for up to about 1 week (［6］).
  • The young are not brightly colored like the parents but brownish and mottled, with brown spots (［6］). Japanese shops also write that the pattern will change as they grow (［18］［19］).
  • The Japanese aquarium moved the newborn young to another tank, because in a large tank they sometimes cannot get enough food (［9］). The Japanese hobbyist also raises them in a separate tank connected to the parents’ tank (［11］).

5. Keeping and breeding

The short answer

Lining up the records, the keys to keeping them are as follows (inference).

  1. A very large tank (240 × 90 × 75 cm or more for 1 adult or 1 pair, ［6］)
  2. Stable water chemistry (［6］［12］［14］)
  3. Warm water of about 25–30 °C (［12］［14］［17］)
  4. Care with the venomous spine (［6］［15］［17］)

FishBase’s 20–25 °C (［5］) is lower than the Japanese keeping guidelines (［12］［14］［17］), so they conflict. What either number is based on has not been checked.

Tank and substrate

  • Seriously Fish says 1 adult or 1 pair needs a tank of 240 × 90 × 75 cm (about 1,620 L) or more (［6］). A keeper in Japan says that an adult with a disc of around 40 cm needs at least 120 × 60 × 60 cm, even on its own (［12］).
  • The substrate is a matter of choice, but river sand is close to nature and is said to be good, especially for young fish. Stingrays bury themselves in sand when startled, so Seriously Fish writes that not providing sand is unkind (［6］).
  • They sometimes come up to the surface, for example when feeding, so keep the lid firmly closed (［6］).

Water chemistry

  • They are said to be sensitive to sudden changes in water chemistry (［14］［17］).
  • A keeper in Japan writes that pH tends to drop when there is a lot of food or the water temperature is high, and that when pH drops they may eat less (［12］).
  • Seriously Fish says they tolerate nitrate to some extent, but not other nitrogenous wastes (［6］).

Food

  • On a Japanese shop’s product page, they are given prepared catfish food, small fish and shrimp (［17］). The Japanese freshwater stingray shop lists live food, frozen shrimp and small fish, prepared foods and so on (［14］).
  • A keeper in Japan normally feeds once a day, and twice a day, morning and evening, in late pregnancy, increasing water changes to once every 2 days to match (［12］).

Tankmates

  • In nature they are top predators, so they cannot be kept with most fish. Fish that are too big to be eaten, peaceful, and swim in the upper layers are said to be suitable (［6］).
  • Catfish with sucker mouths, such as plecos, may attach to the ray’s body and injure it (［6］), or weaken it by rasping at the body surface (［12］).
  • Keeping them with other Potamotrygon is said to be best avoided, because they hybridize easily (［6］).

The venomous spine

  • The tail spine is venomous; Seriously Fish writes that a sting can cause intense pain as well as headache, nausea and so on, and that a doctor should be seen (［6］).
  • The spine is replaced about every 6 months, and venom remains in a shed spine for some time (［6］).
  • A Japanese shop also writes that they are best not handled with bare hands (［17］).

Breeding — steps drawn from the records

Here is a method worth trying, based on the records (inference).

  1. Raise the parents to a large size from a young age. There is a shop record that tank-born fish mature at 1.5–2 years old (［14］). Wild females mature at 5–6 years (［3］)
  2. Pair a female larger than the male, and be ready to separate them if the male keeps biting the female (［6］［7］［9］)
  3. When pregnant, the rear of the back swells (［6］［11］). Feed more, and increase water changes to match (［6］［12］)
  4. Keep the water at 28–30 °C during pregnancy. There is a shop record that low water temperature delays the birth (［14］)
  5. Move the newborn young to another tank. The water becomes cloudy after the birth, so make a larger water change (［6］［9］)

6. Before you buy

What the “Diamond” label means

  • “Diamond” is not a species name but a name for a pattern or bloodline (chapter 2). Many different names are used by different shops and breeders, such as「ダイヤモンドポルカ」(Diamond polka),「ブラックダイヤモンドポルカ」(Black diamond polka),「品川ダイヤ」(Shinagawa diamond),「ビッグスポット」(Big spot),「ギャラクシー」(Galaxy) and「エクリプス」(Eclipse) (［14］［19］［20］).
  • No official definition that sets the standard for these names was found. The Japanese freshwater stingray shop also writes, about how a similar type (the small spot) is divided, that there is no clear boundary (［14］).
  • It is thought to be hard to decide from appearance alone whether a fish is “pure-blood” (chapter 2; inference).

Wild or tank-born?

  • On Japanese shop pages and auctions, labels such as「国産」(domestic),「国内ブリード」(bred in Japan), “WF-1” and “F1” are common (［10］［17］［19］［20］). In the 2025–2026 Japanese sales records seen this time, no newly imported wild fish were found (［19］［20］).
  • A Japanese shop’s product page writes that importing wild fish is prohibited (［17］). However, it does not say which country’s rule, or which rule, this is.
  • A Japanese shop’s blog records wild fish imported from Brazil in 2011 (［10］). This was before the species was listed by CITES (［21］).

Telling the sexes apart

  • Males have a pair of copulatory organs called claspers on the pelvic fins, extending backward like fingers. Even in young males, they can be seen if you look carefully (［6］).

Cautions when introducing them

  • Bones showing as bumps on both sides of the base of the tail are a clear sign of malnutrition. A hollow between the eyes is also said to be a sign of malnutrition (［6］).
  • Fish whose disc edges curl upward are said to rarely recover (［6］).
  • It is said to be best to watch the fish eat at the shop before buying (［6］［12］).
  • Their lifespan is said to sometimes exceed 20 years, so be prepared to keep them for a long time (［6］). A Japanese article gives 10–15 years (［15］).

Legality and regulation

Brazil has set detailed rules for the collection and export of freshwater stingrays. However, it is not known when and how each fish in the trade left the country, so this page cannot state whether the trade in wild fish, or in the original parents of their offspring, was legal. What is known and what is not known are written separately below.

What is known

  • The 2008 freshwater stingray rule (IN IBAMA 204/2008): Brazil allowed ornamental freshwater stingrays to be caught in the states of Amazonas and Pará, only for set species and numbers (annual quotas). For P. leopoldi, it is 5,000 fish a year in Pará, up to a disc width of 30 cm. Only companies holding a quota can sell them, and collecting and trading fish over 30 cm is prohibited (［22］). This rule was made by repealing the 2006 rule (IN IBAMA 118/2006) (［22］).
  • The ornamental fish rule of 2012–2020 (IN 01/2012): it worked by prohibiting the collection of native fish not on its list, but made an exception for fish covered by species-specific rules. It also says that threatened species and species in the CITES Appendices must follow those rules as well (［23］).
  • January 3, 2017: according to the CITES species database (Species+), Brazil listed the whole genus Potamotrygon (Brazilian populations) in Appendix III (［21］). On the same day, Colombia also listed P. motoro, P. magdalenae, P. orbignyi, P. schroederi, P. constellata and others in Appendix III (［21］).
  • Exports after the Appendix III listing: the Brazilian proposal writes that after the Appendix III listing, there were no records of exports of P. leopoldi from Brazil even though it was being caught, and that CITES records showed only exports from Asian countries (［3］).
  • The 2020 ornamental fish rule (IN 10/2020): it changed to a system that permits the collection and trade of native fish except species on the official list of threatened species and species in the CITES Appendices (［24］; an unofficial English translation by a magazine).
  • The 2022 CITES meeting: Brazil submitted a proposal to list P. leopoldi and P. wallacei in Appendix II. It also included the similar P. henlei, P. albimaculata, P. jabuti, P. marquesi and P. signata (［3］).
  • February 23, 2023: according to Species+, P. leopoldi moved to Appendix II. Its earlier Appendix III listing was removed on the same day (［21］). Canada had entered a reservation on this listing, but withdrew it on March 7, 2025 (［21］).
  • Export quota for captive-bred fish: Indonesia has notified CITES of an export quota for live captive-bred P. leopoldi of 104 fish for each year from 2024 to 2026 (［21］).
  • The 2026 list of threatened species (Portaria GM/MMA 1.667/2026): in Brazil’s new list published in the Official Gazette on April 28, 2026, P. leopoldi is No. 358, “Vulnerable (VU)”. There is a “*” next to its number (［25］).
  • IUCN (International Union for Conservation of Nature) Red List: “Vulnerable (VU)” as shown on FishBase, assessed on March 25, 2022 (［5］). The Brazilian proposal (submitted to the 2022 meeting) writes that it is “Data Deficient (DD)” and that a new assessment is under way (［3］).

What is not known

  • Whether the 2008 freshwater stingray rule (［22］) is still in force in 2026 is not known. The 2022 proposal cites this rule as “current law” (［3］), but later amendments or repeal have not been checked.
  • How the treatment of collection and export changed when the species became “Vulnerable (VU)” on the 2026 list is not known. The rule setting out how threatened species are treated (Portaria GM/MMA 1.666/2026) could not be opened and is unverified. The meaning of the “*” on the list was not stated in the document either.
  • Seriously Fish writes that no freshwater stingrays can currently be legally exported from Brazil (［6］), and a UK magazine also wrote in 2009 that Brazil had been restricting exports for several years (［7］). How these statements fit with the quotas of the 2008 rule (［22］) is not known from the sources this time.
  • The Brazilian proposal writes that P. leopoldi larger than the permitted size (a disc width of 35.0 cm) may be being exported as “Potamotrygon spp.”, without the species name (［3］). Why the 2008 rule’s 30 cm (［22］) differs from 35.0 cm is not known.
  • When and where the parents of fish sold in Japan came from is mostly not known, because it is not labeled (［17］［19］［20］). The 2011 import record (［10］) does not say under what documents they were imported either.
  • So it is not known whether the wild fish in the trade, and the original parents of the tank-bred fish, were legally collected and exported.

Japanese regulations: date checked

Checked on 2026-11-18.

  • Invasive Alien Species Act: the Ministry of the Environment’s “List of Invasive Alien Species, etc.” was checked. The only fish on it are some members of the orders Lepisosteiformes (gars), Cypriniformes, Siluriformes, Esociformes, Cyprinodontiformes and Perciformes; stingrays (the family Potamotrygonidae) were not listed as an Invasive Alien Species, an Uncategorized Alien Species, or an organism that requires a species identification certificate (［27］).
  • Act on Welfare and Management of Animals (Specified Animals): on the Ministry of the Environment’s page, the Specified Animals are about 650 species of mammals, birds and reptiles, such as tigers, bears, crocodiles and mamushi (a pit viper) (［28］). The list of Specified Animals also covers only the classes Mammalia, Aves and Reptilia, and fish are not covered (［28］). So even with a venomous spine, no permit under this law is needed (inference).
  • Act on Conservation of Endangered Species (Internationally Endangered Species): in Appended Table 2 of the enforcement order, the only rays and sharks (subclass Elasmobranchii) listed are all species of the family Pristidae (sawfishes) (［29］). This species is not listed.
  • Washington Convention (CITES): this species is in Appendix II (［21］). According to the Customs page, species in Appendix II can be traded for commercial purposes, but an export permit issued by the government of the exporting country is required (［26］).
  • Whether this species is covered by other rules (such as import permits under the Fisheries Resources Protection Act, or prefectural ordinances) has not been checked yet (unverified).

This page’s position

This page does not state whether the wild fish in the trade, or the original parents of their offspring, were legally collected and exported. As above, it sets out what is known and what is not known separately. Even when choosing fish labeled「国産」(domestic) or「ブリード」(bred), please be aware that where the original parents came from is often not known.

7. What is still unknown

Below are the gaps that the sources collected this time could not fill, and the points marked “(unverified)” or “(needs checking)” in the text.

  1. The definition of “Diamond”: no official definition has been found. The only explanations of “spots lined up along the edge of the disc” are the shop website’s explanation of the small spot (［14］) and a blog that wrote it as hearsay (［13］, needs checking).
  2. Whether fish are pure-blood: no record of genetic testing has been found. Whether they can be told apart by appearance is not known either (［11］［15］).
  3. The main text of the describing paper: the 1970 describing paper (［1］) could not be opened. The origin of the species name is also unverified (only an inference from［21］).
  4. The extent of the range: the tributaries named differ between sources: the Iriri and Curuá rivers (［3］) and the Fresco River (［5］［6］).
  5. Water temperature and chemistry of the habitat: no measurements have been found. Where the conflict between FishBase’s 20–25 °C (［5］) and the keeping guideline of 25–30 °C (［12］［14］［17］) comes from is also not known.
  6. Changes in numbers: the effects of the dam and of collecting are described (［3］), but no figure counting how much the numbers have declined has been found.
  7. Number of young in the wild: an average of 4.84 (［3］) and 6.54 (［4］) conflict.
  8. Gestation in captivity: there are records of 10 weeks (［8］) and 3–4 months (［11］) from one birth to the next, but no record counting the time from mating to birth has been found.
  9. Water temperature and chemistry at birth: none of the 5 records has figures.
  10. The UK hobbyist’s record: it is a story that shop staff passed on to a magazine, and the hobbyist’s own record has not been found (［8］, needs checking).
  11. Brazil’s current rules: whether the 2008 freshwater stingray rule (［22］) is still in force, and what changed with the 2026 rule on treatment (Portaria GM/MMA 1.666/2026), are unverified. The meaning of the “*” on the 2026 list is not known either.
  12. Upper size limit for export: why the 2008 rule’s 30 cm (［22］) and the proposal’s 35.0 cm (［3］) differ is not known.
  13. Where the fish in Japan come from: whether they are wild or tank-born, and where the parents came from, are often not known because they are not labeled (［17］［19］［20］).
  14. When the article’s prices applied: when the “several hundred thousand yen even for cheap ones” (［16］) applied is not known.
  15. Japanese regulations: the Fisheries Resources Protection Act and prefectural ordinances are unverified.
  16. Sources that could not be opened: the describing paper (［1］), a document summarizing the keeping and breeding of freshwater stingrays (a PDF in the University of Michigan repository), the site of a Diamond polka breeder in Osaka Prefecture (its name could not be looked up), and an article in the February and March 2018 issues of the monthly magazine Aqua Life (introduced in［14］; not opened).

If you breed the Diamond polka and record the date you saw mating and the date of birth, the water temperature and chemistry at the time, the number and size of the young born, and where the parents came from, that will be very useful as a record that can be compared with others.
