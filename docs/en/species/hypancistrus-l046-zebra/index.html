1. Introduction — how this page was made

The zebra pleco is a small pleco about 7–8 cm long, with black stripes running across a white body (［6］［7］). Japanese articles introduce it as one of the “rare fish”, and write that breeding is difficult and the price is quite high (［20］). About wild fish (fish caught in the wild), one article writes, “Wild fish in particular can fetch around 100,000 yen each, so it can be called a fish of high rarity value.” (translated) (［19］).

It is certain that its country of origin, Brazil, has designated this fish as a threatened species and banned its collection and trade, and that it is listed in an Appendix of the convention on international trade (CITES, the Washington Convention) (［24］［27］［28］). Yet after collecting the records, it turned out that it has been bred in captivity since at least 2000 (［9］), and that there are many breeding records both abroad and in Japan. This page checks “what is rare about it, and what is difficult” against the records.

This page was made by collecting and cross-checking the following kinds of information.

  • Papers and research databases: the 1991 describing paper (［1］; its main text was not opened, and the bibliographic details and abstract were checked in［2］ and elsewhere), a catalog of fish scientific names (［2］), a 2025 paper describing new species of the same genus from the Xingu River (［3］), and a 2025 paper comparing the body shape of 3 species from the same region (［4］)
  • Data sheets: Seriously Fish (［6］), Planet Catfish (［7］), FishBase (［8］)
  • Breeding records from abroad: an article in a US aquarium society’s newsletter (［9］) and breeding reports on Planet Catfish (［10］〜［13］)
  • Records from Japan: hobbyists’ blogs (［15］［17］［18］), a tropical fish shop’s blog (［16］), and articles, shop pages and auction results (［19］〜［23］)
  • Official records: the CITES species database (［24］), Brazil’s rules and lists of threatened species (［25］〜［29］), and pages of Japan Customs and the Ministry of the Environment (［30］［31］)

There are 4 rules for reading this page (the meanings of terms are also collected in the glossary).

  1. Numbers have sources. A ［number］ corresponds to the reference list at the end; clicking it takes you to that reference.
  2. "(needs checking)" marks things such as information picked up from auto-captions or hearsay, which may have been misheard or miswritten.
  3. "(unverified)" marks things whose original source could not be opened and which could not be confirmed.
  4. “Data from a related species” means numbers for fish other than the zebra pleco. The author’s interpretations are marked “(inference)”.

And what is not known is written down as not known. No record that measured the water temperature or water chemistry of the habitat was found in the sources opened this time. The open questions are collected in “7. What is still unknown”.

2. Identity

Described as a genus with a single species

In 1991, I. J. H. Isbrücker and H. Nijssen described a new genus, Hypancistrus, and a new species, H. zebra, based on fish from the Xingu River in Brazil (［1］［2］). At the time of description, this genus contained only this 1 species (the abstract of［1］; ［3］). Since then, species of the same genus have been described one after another from Venezuela, the Rio Negro and elsewhere, and in 2025, 2 species from the Xingu River (H. yudja and H. seideli) were added as well (［2］［3］). The scientific name is the same, Hypancistrus zebra Isbrücker & Nijssen, 1991, in the catalog of fish scientific names (［2］), the CITES species database (［24］), FishBase (［8］) and Brazil’s list of threatened species (［28］).

  • Origin of the name: zebra refers to the stripes of the zebra (［6］［7］). Explanations of the origin of the genus name differ slightly between data sheets (［6］［7］［8］).
  • The specimen the name is based on (holotype): it is kept at the MZUSP in Brazil as number 41668 (［2］).
  • Type locality: in the description, it is a braided stretch of the Xingu River about 1 hour upstream of Altamira by speedboat (［2］). However, Planet Catfish writes that the two authors who described it did not collect at the Xingu River themselves, and that the real place is about 1 hour downstream of Altamira (［7］). Which is correct cannot be confirmed from the sources this time.

Names and L-numbers

When it first began to be imported it was called L046, and it was sold as a member of the Peckoltia group under names such as “Imperial” (［6］). L098 is the number given to a form with a different pattern, whose stripes are wavy or broken (［6］［7］). A 2008 report by Brazil’s Institute of Environment and Renewable Natural Resources (IBAMA) also treats L236 and L250 as regional variants of this species (［29］).

Planet Catfish writes that the person who discovered this fish at the end of 1987 was Yoshio Ogawa, a fish exporter then based in Belém (［7］). The original record is not given, so this is unverified.

Appearance and sexes

  • It has straight black stripes on a white background (［3］). H. yudja from the same river has brown spots and H. seideli a light-and-dark vermiculated pattern, so they can be told apart by the shape of the stripes (［3］).
  • In males, the first thick ray of the pectoral fin (the spine) is thicker, and small spines (odontodes) develop on it. Seen from above, the male’s head looks broader (［6］［7］).
  • Females are thicker around the middle of the body, and the odontodes are not prominent (［7］).
  • The 2025 paper writes that this species has little variation in its pattern, and that the pattern is one of the most distinctive and consistent in the family Loricariidae (［3］).

3. Habitat

Only in the Big Bend of the Xingu River

This species is known only from the Xingu River system in the state of Pará, Brazil (［2］［8］). According to the 2025 paper describing new species, it lives in a stretch of about 160 km of the main river, centered on the Volta Grande do Xingu (the stretch where the Xingu River bends sharply) (［3］; as the authors’ observation). Another paper from the same year gives a stretch of 150 km (［4］). Which is based on more recent surveys is not known from the sources this time.

Since the paper describing new species is based on the authors’ own observations in the field (［3］), shallow rocky areas may be its true home, and catching it in deep places may be due to where or in what season it was collected (inference).

H. yudja from the same river lives in deep places of 15–40 m, and H. seideli at various depths from less than 1 m to 40 m, so they live in different places from this species (［3］).

Water temperature and chemistry — no measurements found

No record that measured the water temperature, pH or electrical conductivity of the habitat was found in the sources opened this time. FishBase gives a water temperature of 23–26 °C (［8］), but the guideline for keeping is 26–30 °C (［6］［7］), so they conflict. What either number is based on has not been checked. Planet Catfish says the water chemistry of the Xingu River is well known, and writes that as long as the water is oxygen-rich and warm, the fish do well in almost any water (［7］).

The Belo Monte Dam

The Belo Monte hydroelectric plant is on the Big Bend of the Xingu River (opened in 2016, ［4］). Two papers describe the dam’s effects on this species’ habitat as follows.

  • The ranges of this species and H. yudja both lie entirely within the area affected by the Belo Monte hydroelectric plant (［3］).
  • The dam changed the river’s flow and severely reduced the dry-season water volume, damaging the rapids and rocky areas essential for breeding and survival. The habitat may shrink further and the risk of extinction may rise (［4］; a statement citing other studies).
  • More than 70% of the Xingu River’s water is diverted for power generation (［3］; stated in the section on the conservation of H. yudja).
  • The dam and recent severe droughts have fragmented the habitat, and hybridization appears to be occurring between H. seideli and H. yudja of the same genus. The body shape of this species was clearly separate from the other 2 species and from the presumed hybrids (［4］).

However, a figure counting how much the numbers of this species have declined since the dam was not found in the sources opened this time. A 2021 paper on its conservation and trade (［5］) could not be opened, so it is unverified.

What the records show when lined up

  • Few eggs: 10–19 eggs per spawn (［11］〜［13］［16］［17］). This roughly matches the data sheets’ “about 7–15” (［6］［7］). There is also a record of about 20 eggs from wild parents but 12–13 from captive-bred parents (［17］).
  • 4–7 days to hatch: 4 days (30–31 °C, ［11］), 5 days (30–32 °C, ［12］), 7 days (26–27 °C, ［13］). The data sheets say “about 1 week” (［6］［7］). The eggs seem to hatch sooner at higher temperatures, but there are only 3 records (inference).
  • Temperature 26–32 °C: tanks where they bred ranged from about 26.7–27.8 °C (［9］) to 30–32 °C (［12］). Japanese records also include 29 °C (［15］) and a recollection that breeding was confirmed at about 27–29 °C (［18］).
  • Water chemistry varies: from soft water at GH 0.5–1 (［13］) to harder water at GH 11–14 (［12］), and from pH 6.5–7.5 (［12］) to 7.2–7.4 (［13］). A Japanese hobbyist writes that they have bred them at pH 7.8 (［18］). Planet Catfish also writes that they breed in all kinds of water, including hard, alkaline water (［7］).
  • Water changes as the trigger: they spawned 2 days after a water change with cold, soft water (［12］) and on the night of a 40–50% water change in the middle of the night (［13］). A Japanese hobbyist also writes that they induce spawning by changing about 80% of the water, and that the new water may be better 1–2 °C cooler (［18］).

Egg size — the records conflict

Egg diameters are recorded as 4 mm (［9］), 4.0 mm (［11］) and 2.0 mm (［13］). How they were measured is not stated, so which is correct is not known.

The first spawnings often fail

  • In the US record, the 1st spawning was infertile (［9］). Planet Catfish also writes that first spawnings are “trials” and that the eggs often do not develop (［7］).
  • In a Japanese shop’s record, some eggs of a pair spawning for the first time were put out of the tube every day (August 2017, ［16］), and at the previous spawning of a young pair in 2019, the male seemed to have eaten the fry right after they hatched (［16］).
  • The Japanese shop writes several times that few fry survive from a first spawning (posts of December 2016, February 2017 and August 2017, ［16］).

Artificial hatching, or leave it to the male?

Some people leave the eggs to the male (usually in［15］, and in most cases in［16］), and some take the eggs out (［13］, and［15］ in 2012). When the Japanese shop tried artificial hatching, only 3 of 13 grew up, and many fry died unable to absorb their yolk sacs (the sac of nutrients on the belly) (［16］). On the other hand, a Japanese hobbyist hatched them artificially in fresh water at 32 °C and got 23 fish (［15］). With so few records, which is better is not known.

5. Keeping and breeding

The short answer

Lining up the records, the keys to keeping them are as follows (inference).

  1. Warm water of 26–30 °C (［6］［7］)
  2. A strong current and oxygen-rich water (［6］［7］)
  3. Hiding places just big enough for the body to fit into (tubes or crevices in stones, ［6］［7］［18］)
  4. Mainly meaty foods (［6］［7］)

The Japanese article’s［20］ “prefers lower water temperatures” does not match any of the other sources (［6］［7］［9］［11］〜［13］［15］［18］).

Tank and hiding places

  • Scattering smooth pebbles and rocks large and small over a sand bottom and moving the water with a powerhead brings the tank close to the natural environment (［6］). Piling rocks up casually to make lots of crevices and ledges is said to be good (［7］).
  • Tubes whose entrance is slightly larger than the fish’s body are said to be best, ideally with water flowing nearby (［6］). A Japanese hobbyist writes that the fish did not like tubes clearly larger than their bodies, and that many preferred tubes that narrow toward the end (［18］).
  • Plants do not grow easily in this environment, but Anubias or Microsorum may last for a while (［6］).

Food

  • Except when young, they prefer meaty foods such as bloodworms, chopped shellfish and shrimp, and sinking prepared foods. They do not eat much algae or vegetables (［6］［7］).
  • The people who bred them gave frozen bloodworms and brine shrimp, pellets, paste foods and the like (［11］〜［13］).

Tankmates

  • They are peaceful but tend to lose out in fierce competition for food, and they need high temperatures, oxygen and clean water, so they are said not to suit general community tanks (［6］).
  • They fight over territory with their own species and similar plecos, so it is said to be best to make this species the only bottom dweller (［6］). A group of 1 male to 2–3 females is recommended (［6］［7］).

Breeding — steps drawn from the records

Here is a method worth trying, based on the records (inference).

  1. Raise 6 or more together and wait for pairs to form (［6］). A guideline for breeding is males of about 7 cm and females of a little over 6 cm (［18］)
  2. Place several tubes that the body just fits into, and also make places nearby where the females can wait (［6］［18］)
  3. Keep the water at around 27–30 °C, with plenty of current and oxygen (［6］［7］［18］)
  4. Induce spawning with large water changes using cold water (［6］［12］［13］［18］). Seriously Fish suggests repeating 50–70% cold water changes every 2–3 days (［6］). There is also a record that raising the temperature with water changes can stop spawning (［18］)
  5. When the male puts only his head into the tube and leaves his body outside to call the female, breeding is near (［17］)
  6. Leave the eggs to the male, or take them out and hatch them while keeping them moving with air (［6］［13］［15］)
  7. The fry do not feed while the yolk sac remains (［9］). Once it is gone, keep brine shrimp nauplii or sinking prepared foods available so they can eat at any time (［6］)

However, the records differ on the trigger. One Japanese hobbyist changes the water the day before the spring tides (the days around the full and new moon when the tides are large) (［18］). Another hobbyist writes that just changing the water the day before the spring tides does not make them spawn (［17］).

6. Before you buy

What is “rare” about it?

Japanese articles introduce this species as one of the “rare fish” (［20］) and as a fish of high rarity value (［19］). Lining up the dated sources shows that what is rare is wild fish, and that captive-bred fish are bred and sold both in Japan and abroad.

A UK data sheet writes that even captive-bred fish cost over 100 pounds, and that proven breeding adults cost more (no date, ［6］).

Wild or captive-bred?

  • A Japanese article says the fish traded in Japan are captive-bred (［21］). On the other hand, the Japanese shop’s blog from 2015–2021 mentions wild parents and wild fish that had been kept for a long time (［16］).
  • None of the sources say whether these are fish imported before Brazil banned exports in 2004, or fish that came in after that.

Telling the sexes apart

  • Males have a thicker first pectoral-fin ray with well-developed odontodes, and a broader head (［6］［7］). Odontodes also appear on the cheeks and the rear of the body (［7］)
  • Females are thicker around the middle of the body, and the odontodes are not prominent (［7］)
  • The odontodes on a male’s pectoral fins catch easily in nets, so take care when netting them (［6］)

Cautions when introducing them

  • Check that the belly is rounded and the eyes are not sunken. These are said to be the signs of thinness in newly imported fish (［6］).
  • Small fry take time to grow (［16］). The Japanese shop writes that their bodies become sturdy at around 4 cm (［16］).

Legality and regulation

In its country of origin, Brazil, collecting and trading this species is prohibited because it is a threatened species. International trade is also regulated under CITES Appendix II. However, it is not known when and how each fish in the trade left the country, so this page cannot state whether the trade in wild fish was legal. What is known and what is not known are written separately below.

What is known

  • The 2004 list of threatened species: IBAMA’s 2008 report writes that this species was added to the list of threatened species in a rule of Brazil’s Ministry of the Environment (IN MMA 05/2004) (［29］). Seriously Fish writes that the Brazilian government banned its export in 2004 (［6］).
  • The ornamental fish rule of 2012–2020 (IN 01/2012): it listed the native fish that could be collected and sold as ornamental fish (Annex I), and prohibited native species not on the list. L-number species of the same genus (L066, L333 and others) are on it, but this species is not (［25］).
  • The 2014 list of threatened species (Portaria MMA 445/2014): this species is No. 126, “Critically Endangered (CR)”. Species in this category are fully protected, and their collection, transport, storage, trade and so on are prohibited. However, the prohibition is said not to apply to captive-bred specimens that are formally authorized by the competent environmental agency and, where there is a national conservation action plan (PAN), in line with that plan (Article 2, ［27］).
  • The 2020 ornamental fish rule (IN 10/2020): it changed to a system that permits the collection and trade of native fish except species on the official list of threatened species and species in the CITES Appendices (［26］; an unofficial English translation by a magazine).
  • The 2026 list of threatened species (Portaria GM/MMA 1.667/2026): in the new list published in the Official Gazette on April 28, 2026, this species is again listed, as No. 218, “Critically Endangered (CR)”. This list repealed the 2014 list (［28］).
  • CITES: according to the CITES species database (Species+), it was listed in Appendix III at Brazil’s request on January 3, 2017, and moved to Appendix II on February 23, 2023. The Appendix II listing carries the condition “commercial export of wild specimens: 0” (［24］). Brazil’s 2026 export quota is also 0 for commercial export of wild specimens (［24］).
  • How it came to be in Appendix II: according to a US magazine article, at the 2022 CITES meeting Brazil proposed listing it in Appendix I, but there were not enough votes, and at the end of the meeting an amendment to list it in Appendix II was adopted (［14］; an article in a trade-leaning magazine).
  • IUCN (International Union for Conservation of Nature) Red List: “Critically Endangered (CR)” as shown on FishBase, assessed on November 7, 2018 (［8］). Planet Catfish also gives CR, assessed in 2018 (［7］).

What is not known

  • The text of the 2004 rule (IN MMA 05/2004) has not been opened, so when and under which article the export ban began is unverified (［6］［29］).
  • The rule issued together with the 2026 list that sets out how threatened species are treated (Portaria GM/MMA 1.666/2026) could not be opened, so whether the treatment of captive-bred specimens has changed from the 2014 rule is unverified.
  • A US magazine writes that under Brazilian law it is not permitted to keep this species, even for breeding (［14］). Article 2 of the 2014 rule has an exception for authorized captive breeding (［27］), and whether this fits the magazine’s account is not known from the sources this time.
  • The proposal to the CITES meeting (CoP19 Prop. 41) could not be opened as a PDF, so it is unverified. The account of the move to Appendix II is based only on the magazine article (［14］) and the Species+ record (［24］).
  • For the IUCN Red List, FishBase and Planet Catfish give CR (［7］［8］), while the 2025 paper says “Endangered (EN)” (［4］), so they conflict. The IUCN page itself has not been opened and is unverified.
  • When and where the parents of the fish sold in Japan came from is not known, because it is not labeled (［16］［22］［23］). How the parents of fish labeled “WF1” and the like left the country is not known either.
  • So it is not known whether the wild fish in the trade, and the original parents of the fish bred from wild parents, were legally collected and exported.

Japanese regulations: date checked

Checked on 2026-11-18.

  • Invasive Alien Species Act: the Ministry of the Environment’s “List of Invasive Alien Species, etc.” was checked. The only fish of the order Siluriformes (catfishes) on it are some members of the Bagridae, the Ictaluridae (the channel catfish group) and the Siluridae; neither this species nor the family Loricariidae was listed as an Invasive Alien Species, an Uncategorized Alien Species, or an organism that requires a species identification certificate (［31］).
  • Washington Convention (CITES): this species is in Appendix II (［24］). According to the Customs page, species in Appendix II can be traded for commercial purposes, but an export permit issued by the government of the exporting country is required (［30］). For this species, Brazil’s export quota for commercial export of wild specimens is 0 (［24］).
  • Whether this species is covered by other laws (such as the Act on Conservation of Endangered Species) has not been checked yet (unverified).

This page’s position

This page does not state whether the wild fish in the trade were legally collected and exported. As above, it sets out what is known and what is not known separately. Even when choosing captive-bred fish, please be aware that where the parents came from is often not known.

7. What is still unknown

Below are the gaps that the sources collected this time could not fill, and the points marked “(unverified)” or “(needs checking)” in the text.

  1. The main text of the describing paper: the 1991 describing paper (［1］) could not be opened. Only the bibliographic details and the abstract were checked.
  2. Type locality: whether the description’s “upstream of Altamira” or Planet Catfish’s “actually downstream” (［7］) is correct is not known.
  3. Length of the range: about 160 km (［3］) and 150 km (［4］) conflict.
  4. Depth of the habitat: the papers say shallow places (［3］［4］) and the data sheets deep places (［6］［7］), which conflict.
  5. Water temperature and chemistry of the habitat: no measurements have been found. Where the conflict between FishBase’s 23–26 °C (［8］) and the keeping guideline of 26–30 °C (［6］［7］) comes from is also not known.
  6. Changes in numbers since the dam: it is written that the species lives within the area affected by the dam (［3］［4］), but no figure counting how much the numbers have declined has been found. The 2021 paper on conservation and trade (［5］) is unverified.
  7. Egg size: 4 mm (［9］［11］) and 2.0 mm (［13］) conflict.
  8. The relationship between days to hatch and water temperature: hatching seems faster at higher temperatures, but there are only 3 records (［11］〜［13］, inference).
  9. The “Number” field in the Planet Catfish reports: what it counts (fry hatched, fry raised, and so on) is not stated (［11］［13］). The combination of GH 0.5–1 and KH 9–17.9 in pointpleco’s record is unnatural and may be a mistake (［13］, inference).
  10. Lifespan: an article says its author saw information that fish kept for more than 10 years are still breeding (［18］), but the original record is not known (needs checking).
  11. Brazil’s current rules: the text of the 2004 rule and the 2026 rule on treatment (Portaria GM/MMA 1.666/2026) are unverified. The proposal to the CITES meeting is also unverified.
  12. IUCN assessment: CR (［7］［8］) and EN (［4］) conflict. The IUCN page is unverified.
  13. History of discovery: the original record behind the story that Yoshio Ogawa discovered it in 1987 (［7］) is unverified.
  14. Where the fish in Japan come from: whether they are wild or captive-bred, and where the parents came from, are not known because they are not labeled (［16］［22］［23］).
  15. Japanese regulations: the Act on Conservation of Endangered Species and others are unverified.
  16. Sources that could not be opened: the paper on conservation and trade (［5］) could not be opened because ScienceDirect showed a human-verification screen. The PDF of the paper on the body length and weight of fish from the Xingu River (Barros et al. 2023) could not be opened.

If you breed the zebra pleco and record the water temperature and water chemistry at spawning, the trigger, the number and size of the eggs, the days to hatch, and how the fry grow, that will be very useful as a record that can be compared with others.
