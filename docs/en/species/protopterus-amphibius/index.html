1. Introduction — how this page was made

Protopterus amphibius, the gilled lungfish, is one of the African lungfishes. A Japanese article introduces it as the smallest species in this group, native to the coast of East Africa, and writes, “Only a few juveniles are imported when the season comes, so it is rarer than other lungfishes.” (translated) (［19］).

This page checks, going back to the original description of 1844, whether that “rarity” can be confirmed from the records, and what kind of fish it is in the first place.

This page was made by collecting and cross-checking the following kinds of information.

  • The original description and papers: the 1844 original description (［1］), a catalog of fish scientific names (［2］), FishBase (［3］［4］), the IUCN Red List (［5］), a database of the origins of scientific names (［6］), a review of the African lungfishes (［7］), experiments on breathing and aestivation (［8］〜［10］), papers that examined imported fish (［11］［12］), a paper on the fish of a Kenyan lake (［13］), and a survey of the breeding of a related species (［14］)
  • Articles and forums from abroad: the website of a German ornamental fish importer (［15］) and a US forum for keepers of large fish (［16］［17］)
  • Records from Japan: a keeper’s blog (［18］), articles and blogs (［19］〜［22］), shop pages (［23］［24］), and auction results (［25］)
  • Records of regulations: the CITES species database (［26］), pages of the Ministry of the Environment and Japanese law (［27］〜［30］), and Kenya’s fisheries law (［31］)

There are 4 rules for reading this page (the meanings of terms are also collected in the glossary).

  1. Numbers have sources. A ［number］ corresponds to the reference list at the end; clicking it takes you to that reference.
  2. "(needs checking)" marks things such as hearsay or forum posts, which may have been misheard or miswritten.
  3. "(unverified)" marks things whose original source could not be opened and which could not be confirmed.
  4. “Data from a related species” means numbers for fish other than Protopterus amphibius (such as P. annectens). The author’s interpretations are marked “(inference)”.

And what is not known is written down as not known. No record of breeding in captivity was found, either abroad or in Japan. No record that measured the water temperature or water chemistry of the habitat has been found either. Whether the Kenyan population really is this species is not clear. The open questions are collected in “7. What is still unknown”.

2. Identity

The original description of 1844

This fish was first reported at a meeting of the Berlin Academy of Sciences on December 5, 1844. J. Müller read out a report by W. Peters (［1］). The original is in German, and the translations below are by the author (AI).

  • The report was about a fish from the swamps of Quelimane, related to Lepidosiren annectens, that has lungs and gills at the same time (［1］). Quelimane is a town in Mozambique (［2］).
  • It says that in the dry season this fish stays underground in a covering of leaves (［1］).
  • Because it closely resembled the already known West African lungfish (today’s P. annectens), Peters thought it might be the same species. He then proposed that, if the genus was different, it should be named Rhinocryptis amphibia (［1］).
  • The differences he gave were the cartilaginous rays of the pectoral and pelvic fins, the cartilage of the lips, the structure of the nostrils, and gills sticking out of the body (external gills) (［1］).

The external gills are described in detail (［1］).

  • There are 3 external gills, lined up vertically behind the gill opening, above the pectoral fin. They are unbranched and look like tentacles.
  • Two are the same length, 4 lines (Linien), and the lowest one is much shorter.
  • They are present in all fish, not only young ones but also adults of 2 feet (Fuß).

Converted using the Prussian units of the time, 2 feet is about 63 cm and 4 lines about 9 mm (inference; values converted by the author, and the original does not say which units were used). This figure of about 63 cm is larger than FishBase’s maximum total length of 44.3 cm (［3］), so they conflict.

  • The parentheses in “(Peters, 1844)” after the scientific name show that the genus has changed since the description. The ending of the species name changing from amphibia to amphibius is thought to be to match the genus name (inference).
  • Peters put the name forward on the condition “if” the genus was different, but the catalog of fish scientific names treats this 1844 page 414 as the description (［2］).

The Kenyan population may be a different species

FishBase’s Kenya page, based on a 2003 checklist of Kenyan freshwater fishes (Seegers et al.), says that the classification of the Kenyan population is unclear, and it is likely to be a species different from P. amphibius (［4］). The catalog of fish scientific names also notes that this checklist treated it as “aff. amphibius” (close to amphibius) (［2］). For this reason, FishBase marks its distribution in Kenya as “questionable” (［4］).

The main text of the original 2003 checklist has not been opened, so where the differences are said to lie is unverified. Some fish traded in Japan are labeled「ケニア産」(from Kenya) (［25］), and the fish used in the South Korean study were also imported from Kenya (［11］). Many of the “amphibius” sold in Japan may be these Kenyan fish of unclear classification (inference).

Origin of the name

The database of the origins of scientific names gives the species name as meaning a double life, combining “both” and “life”. It says that the origin is not explained in the original description, and that it may refer to the view at the time that lungfishes were amphibians, or to living in the water in the rainy season and in a covering of leaves in the dry season (［6］).

How to tell it from similar species

  • There are 4 species of African lungfish. The slender P. dolloi is easy to tell apart, but for the other 3 — P. aethiopicus, P. annectens and P. amphibius — the position where the dorsal fin begins is said to be the reliable way to tell them apart. In P. amphibius, the dorsal fin begins just behind the pectoral fins (［15］).
  • FishBase gives the distance from the tip of the snout to the start of the dorsal fin as 1.4–1.7 times the head length, or 45–56% of the distance from the tip of the snout to the anus (［3］).
  • The South Korean study says that, while P. dolloi is dark brown, this species has a uniformly blue body with a pale grey belly (［11］). A Japanese article writes that body color varies greatly between individuals, from yellowish to blackish (［20］).

The IUCN Red List treats Kenya, Mozambique and Somalia as its native range and Tanzania as “introduced” (［5］). FishBase says that the Tanzanian records have not been confirmed (［3］), so the sources treat it differently.

Where it lives

  • FishBase says it lives in swamps and floodplains (［3］). The IUCN also gives its habitat as inland wetlands (［5］).
  • The 1844 original description reports it as a fish from the “swamps” of Quelimane (［1］).
  • A 1986 review of the 4 African lungfishes writes that this group lives in both still and flowing water, and often settles in places where other fish cannot live (［7］).

Water temperature and chemistry — no measurements found

No record that measured the water temperature, pH or electrical conductivity of the habitat was found in the sources opened this time. What the keeping guidelines (chapter 5) are based on is not known either.

How it survives the dry season (aestivation)

  • The original description says that in the dry season it stays underground in a covering of leaves (［1］).
  • FishBase says that lungfishes survive for months under the dried mud of floodplains (［3］; a statement about lungfishes in general).
  • In experiments, there is a record of examining the blood of fish made to aestivate for 28–30 months. During aestivation, the proportion of red blood cells in the blood and the amount of oxygen it could carry had increased by about 50%, and its ability to bind oxygen had become stronger (［9］). The same research group also writes that it used fish that had aestivated for about 30 months (［10］). Where and how the fish were made to aestivate is not stated in the abstracts.
  • The 1986 review says that all 4 species can survive long dry seasons, in various ways, such as making a cocoon underground, hiding in a burrow holding water, or burrowing into a moist bottom (［7］). Which way this species uses is not known from the sources this time.

Breathing with air and with gills

  • This species is said to be a fish that cannot live without breathing air (an obligate air-breather) (［3］).
  • In a 1976 experiment, the amounts of oxygen taken in from air and from water were measured per body weight. The smallest fish (written “3-7 g” in the abstract) took 70% of the oxygen it needed from the water, but larger fish (up to 255 g) took only 10–15% (［8］).
  • In other words, while small it relies more on breathing in the water (with its gills), and as it grows it comes to rely on breathing air (［8］). How this relates to keeping external gills into adulthood (［1］) is not stated in the abstract.

Local use

The IUCN says it is eaten locally (［5］). FishBase’s Kenya page says it is exported live (［4］).

4. Breeding records compared

No breeding records have been found yet.

No record of spawning or of raising fry in captivity was found, either abroad or in Japan.

  • The 1986 review says that 3 of the 4 African lungfishes build nests and care for their young, but about this species writes, “The breeding biology of the fourth species, P. amphibius, is still unknown.” (［7］).
  • In 2006, the administrator of a US forum wrote that this species had not yet bred in captivity, and that almost nothing was known about how it spawns in the wild either (［16］; needs checking).
  • In 2024, on the same forum, there was a post about African lungfish (the species was not stated) saying that there had been reports of spawning from Japan and South Korea, but that the poster thought none had succeeded in hatching (［17］; hearsay, so needs checking).

What the lack of records suggests

  • The lack of breeding records may be related to the small number of imports, and to their aggressiveness, which makes it hard to keep 2 together (inference). A 2024 forum post also says that, given their aggressiveness and low price, probably no one has seriously tried (［17］; needs checking).
  • If, like related species, it spawns in an underground nest guarded by the male, it may need something like a large pond with a muddy bottom rather than a tank (inference).

5. Keeping and breeding

The short answer

Lining up the records, the keys to keeping them are as follows (inference).

  1. Keep it alone (［20］［21］［23］)
  2. A lid with no gaps, and air at the surface (as chapter 3 shows, it cannot live without breathing air; ［3］)
  3. Do not let the water get too dirty (many sources say it is rather sensitive to worsening water quality; ［20］［21］［23］)
  4. Be ready to keep it for many years (records of lifespan differ widely between sources; see the table below)

As there are no measurements from the habitat (chapter 3), the water temperature and chemistry are guidelines of the “people kept it successfully like this” kind (inference). How often to change the water differs widely between sources, from once a month (［20］) to once a week (［21］).

Size

  • FishBase’s maximum is 44.3 cm total length (［3］). A Japanese shop gives a maximum of around 45 cm (［23］), one Japanese article 40–50 cm (［20］), and another about 50 cm at most (［21］).
  • A Japanese blog writes that in captivity it grows only to just under 60 cm (［22］). Considering the original description’s “2-foot adults” (converted to about 63 cm; inference) (［1］), it may not be safe to say that it stops at around 45 cm (inference).
  • A Japanese article writes that growth is known to slow once it reaches about 25 cm, but gives no original record (［20］; needs checking).

Food

  • The fish at a Japanese shop ate「キャット」(Cat, a prepared food for catfish) and shellfish (［23］).
  • A Japanese article introduces a method of starting with live food (medaka, frozen bloodworms, small goldfish, shrimp and so on) and getting it used to prepared food by transferring the smell (［21］).
  • A Japanese keeper writes that the fish ate prepared food from the first day home from the shop, and that large pellets were broken into quarters to sixths. The keeper also writes that it chews food once, spits it out, and eats it again (［18］).
  • A Japanese article writes that once a day is enough while it is growing and once every 2–3 days after it has grown, and that fish are sometimes killed by overfeeding (［20］).

Tankmates and escapes

  • It is strongly territorial and fights fiercely with its own kind and with other fish, so keeping it alone is recommended (［20］).
  • It is powerful and likes to squeeze into gaps, so there is a warning about it shifting a filter pump and making the water overflow (［20］). It also has a strong bite, so heaters and other equipment should be hidden, and care taken not to get your hand bitten (［20］).

Breeding

There are no records from captivity, so no steps can be given. If you try, the records of related species (chapter 4) suggest some clues to think about: a muddy bottom where it can make an underground nest, changes in water level that imitate the rainy season, and a place where the male can guard the eggs (inference).

6. Before you buy

Is it really a rare fish?

A Japanese article writes that only a few juveniles come in each season, and that imports were at one time interrupted (［19］). Another article says it is not unusual for it to arrive only once every few years (［21］).

Labels of origin

  • At auction, besides「ケニア産」(from Kenya), there were fish labeled「ナイバシャ湖」(Lake Naivasha) (［25］).
  • However, the list of the fish of Lake Naivasha given in a 2008 Kenyan paper (native fish and introduced fish) does not include any lungfish (［13］). The places given for this species’ distribution are also the lower reaches of coastal rivers, the Ewaso Ng’iro in the north and so on (［4］). What the label “Lake Naivasha” refers to (where it was caught, an export base, or something else) is not known (needs checking).
  • The Kenyan population is said to be likely a different species (chapter 2; ［4］).

Telling the sexes apart

No record that looked at how to tell the sexes apart in this species was found. In P. annectens, a study of wild fish says that males have longer, broader heads (［15］; data from a related species).

Cautions when introducing them

  • A Japanese shop writes that it has a rather stubby body shape and swims actively (［23］).
  • The fish at a shop in 2023 is described as having damaged fins (［24］).
  • With newly imported fish, it is best to watch first whether they eat (inference; the Japanese keeper’s fish ate from the first day; ［18］).

Legality and regulation

This species is not listed by CITES (the Washington Convention), and, as far as was checked this time, it is not among the species whose import or keeping is regulated by Japanese law either. However, it is not known in which country, or under which permits, each fish in the trade was collected and exported, so this page cannot state whether the trade in wild fish was legal. What is known and what is not known are written separately below.

What is known

  • CITES: searching the species database of the CITES Secretariat and others (Species+) for Protopterus (genus) and Protopteridae (family) gave no records (checked November 18, 2026) (［26］). FishBase also shows “Not Evaluated” (［3］). The Australian lungfish (Neoceratodus forsteri), another lungfish, is in Species+ (［26］).
  • IUCN Red List: “Least Concern (LC)”. It was assessed on May 1, 2009 and published in 2010, with a note that it “needs updating” (［5］). The population trend is “unknown” (［5］).
  • Threats listed by the IUCN: agriculture, fishing, dams and water management, and pollution from domestic and agricultural wastewater (［5］).
  • Kenyan law: the fisheries law (Fisheries Management and Development Act, Cap. 378; the version as of July 10, 2026) allows live fish to be taken out of the country only in accordance with regulations. Violations carry a fine of up to 500,000 shillings, imprisonment of up to 3 years, or both (［31］).
  • Records of export from Kenya: fish imported from Kenya into South Korea on August 26, 2020 were used in research (［11］). FishBase’s Kenya page also says that it is exported live (［4］).

What is not known

  • What permits the “regulations” referred to in Kenyan law require for collecting and exporting freshwater ornamental fish has not been checked (unverified).
  • The collecting and export rules of Somalia, Mozambique and Tanzania have not been checked (unverified).
  • From which country, and with what documents, the fish sold in Japan were imported is mostly not known, because it is not labeled (［23］［24］［25］). What backs up the labels「ケニア産」(from Kenya) and「ナイバシャ湖」(Lake Naivasha) (［25］) is not known either.
  • Whether its treatment would change if the Kenyan population were treated as a different species is not known (inference; it is currently not listed under any regulation).
  • So it is not known whether the wild fish in the trade were legally collected and exported.

Japanese regulations: date checked

Checked on 2026-11-18.

  • Invasive Alien Species Act: the Ministry of the Environment’s “List of Invasive Alien Species, etc.” was checked. The only fish on it are some members of the orders Lepisosteiformes (gars), Cypriniformes, Siluriformes, Esociformes, Cyprinodontiformes and Perciformes; lungfishes were not listed as an Invasive Alien Species, an Uncategorized Alien Species, or an organism that requires a species identification certificate (［27］).
  • List of Alien Species That May Damage Ecosystems (not a legal regulation, but a list to call for caution): lungfishes were not in the file listing fish (［28］).
  • Act on Welfare and Management of Animals (Specified Animals): on the Ministry of the Environment’s page, the Specified Animals are about 650 species of mammals, birds and reptiles, such as tigers, bears, crocodiles and mamushi (a pit viper) (［29］). The list of Specified Animals also covers only the classes Mammalia, Aves and Reptilia, and fish are not covered (［29］).
  • Act on Conservation of Endangered Species: in the text of the enforcement order (e-Gov law search), the name of lungfishes (the genus Protopterus) was not found (［30］).
  • Washington Convention (CITES): as above, this species is not listed (［26］).
  • Whether this species is covered by other rules (such as import permits under the Fisheries Resources Protection Act, or prefectural ordinances) has not been checked yet (unverified).

This page’s position

This page does not state whether the wild fish in the trade were legally collected and exported. As above, it sets out what is known and what is not known separately. Not being regulated under Japanese law does not mean that collecting and export in the country of origin were legal.

7. What is still unknown

Below are the gaps that the sources collected this time could not fill, and the points marked “(unverified)” or “(needs checking)” in the text.

  1. What the Kenyan population really is: it is said to be likely a different species (［4］), but the main text of the 2003 checklist that says how it differs has not been opened (unverified). Whether most of the fish coming into Japan are from Kenya is not known either (inference).
  2. Conflicting sizes: FishBase’s 44.3 cm (［3］), the original description’s “2-foot adults” (about 63 cm; the conversion is inference) (［1］) and a Japanese blog’s “just under 60 cm” (［22］) do not agree.
  3. Water temperature and chemistry of the habitat: no measurements have been found. What the keeping guidelines (［20］［21］［23］) are based on is not known either.
  4. How Tanzania is treated: the sources differ, with “not confirmed” (［3］) and “introduced” (［5］).
  5. How it aestivates: no record has been found describing how this species aestivates in the wild, such as whether it makes a cocoon or hides in a burrow. Only the abstracts of the experimental papers (［8］〜［10］) have been read.
  6. Breeding: no breeding record of this species has been found, in captivity or in the wild (［7］). The reports of spawning in Japan and South Korea are hearsay and need checking (［17］).
  7. Telling the sexes apart: there is no record for this species.
  8. Lifespan: about 7 years (［21］), 9 years (［18］) and 20–30 years (［20］) differ widely.
  9. Growth: what lies behind the statement that growth slows at 25 cm (［20］) is not known (needs checking).
  10. Labels of origin: what the label “Lake Naivasha” (［25］) means is not known (needs checking).
  11. Rules in the countries of origin: Kenya’s rules for exporting freshwater ornamental fish (the “regulations” referred to in［31］) and the rules of Somalia, Mozambique and Tanzania are unverified.
  12. Japanese regulations: the Fisheries Resources Protection Act and prefectural ordinances are unverified.
  13. Sources that could not be opened: the checklist of Kenyan freshwater fishes (2003; the PDF on BioOne could not be obtained), the main text of the 1976 paper on breathing (abstract only), the main text of the 1986 paper on liver tumors (abstract only), and the product page of a major Japanese online shop (it could not be opened because it was under maintenance).

If you keep P. amphibius and record the origin label, its size when you started keeping it and now, the water temperature and chemistry, how many years it lived, and how it behaved when placed in dry conditions, that will be very useful as a record that can be compared with others.
