1. Introduction — how this page was made

The Peru glass tetra is a small characin whose body is as clear as glass. A Japanese article introduces it as native to Peru, about 4 cm long, hardy and easy to keep, and then writes, “Like other rare tropical fish, it is a species that is difficult to breed.” (translated), presenting this as the reason its price is on the high side (［9］).

This page first checks which fish the name “Peru glass tetra” refers to, and then examines whether the claims that it is “hard to breed” and “expensive” can be confirmed from the records.

This page was made by collecting and cross-checking the following kinds of information.

  • Papers and research databases: the 1978 original description (［1］), the 2016 redescription and description of a new genus (［2］), a catalog of fish scientific names (［3］［5］), and FishBase (［4］)
  • Articles from abroad: pages of a German ornamental fish importer (［6］〜［8］)
  • Articles and guides from Japan: articles (［9］〜［11］［16］) and descriptions on shop and fish guide sites (［12］〜［15］)
  • Keeping and trade records from Japan: online shop product pages and buyers’ reviews (［17］［19］), shop blogs and pages (［18］［20］〜［27］), and auction results (［28］)
  • Records of regulations: the CITES species database (［29］), pages of the Ministry of the Environment, Japanese law and the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (［30］〜［34］), and a Peruvian guide to prohibited species (［35］)

There are 4 rules for reading this page (the meanings of terms are also collected in the glossary).

  1. Numbers have sources. A ［number］ corresponds to the reference list at the end; clicking it takes you to that reference.
  2. "(needs checking)" marks things such as hearsay or short impressions, which may have been misheard or miswritten.
  3. "(unverified)" marks things whose original source could not be opened and which could not be confirmed.
  4. “Data from a related species” means numbers for fish other than Protocheirodon pi. The author’s interpretations are marked “(inference)”.

And what is not known is written down as not known. No record of breeding in captivity was found, either abroad or in Japan. No record measuring the water temperature or water chemistry of the habitat has been found either. The open questions are collected in “7. What is still unknown”.

2. Identity

Conclusion — a trade name for the described species Protocheirodon pi (with exceptions in some labels)

To give the conclusion first: the fish called “Peru glass tetra” in Japan is, in almost all sources that give a scientific name, Protocheirodon pi, described in 1978 (as Leptagoniates pi at the time of description). (［1］［2］ and the table below) It is not an undescribed species (sp.), nor the name of a type within a species.

However, the way the scientific name is written differs between sources, and some sources give a scientific name that is not this species.

  • Phenacogaster pectinatus, which the online shop’s product page lists with a “?”, is, in the catalog of fish scientific names, Phenacogaster pectinata, a valid species of a different subfamily (Characinae), described in 1870 from Pebas, Peru (［5］). It is a different fish from Protocheirodon pi.
  • The article that gives Aphyocharax alburnus (［16］) does not give its basis. The π-shaped swim bladder described in “How to identify it” below is said to be a feature found only in P. pi among the Characiformes (［2］).
  • When the 2 fish guide sites that give “Characidae sp.” (［14］［15］) were written is not known. They may have been written before the match between the trade name and the scientific name became known (inference).

In other words, although the match between the name and the scientific name is mostly settled on P. pi, it is not the same in every source. The reliable way to check whether a fish that arrives is really P. pi is to look for the features in “How to identify it” below (inference).

Other trade names that sound similar

There are other trade names that include “Peru” and “glass”.

  • The blog of a shop that has imported the Peru glass tetra many times (［21］) lists「ペルビアンドワーフグラステトラ」(Peruvian dwarf glass tetra) and「ペルビアングラスダータテトラ」(Peruvian glass darter tetra) with prices, as separate fish (arrivals of September 8, 2023; ［22］).
  • Another shop’s blog introduces「ペルビアンイエローエッジテトラ（レモンフィングラステトラ）」(Peruvian yellow-edge tetra (Lemon-fin glass tetra)) separately from the Peru glass tetra (January 28, 2020; ［23］).

What these fish are has not been investigated on this page. The point is that searching by name alone may lead you to a different fish (inference).

  • Why the genus changed: in the original description, Vari himself wrote that placing this fish in the genus Leptagoniates was a provisional treatment to fit the way genera had been defined until then (［1］). The 2016 paper showed, through a phylogenetic analysis of DNA and body structure, that this species belongs to a different subfamily from the other species of Leptagoniates, and created the new genus Protocheirodon (［2］).
  • Origin of the name: the species name pi comes from the shape of the swim bladder, which resembles the Greek letter π (pi) (［1］). The genus name combines a Greek word meaning “first” with the name of the genus Cheirodon, referring to this species being the sister group of all other members of the Cheirodontinae (［2］).
  • A 2025 article by the German importer gives the year of description as 1979 (［8］), but in the original description and the catalog it is 1978 (［1］［3］).

How to identify it — the “π” swim bladder seen through the body

This species has a feature said to be found only in this species among the Characiformes (［1］［2］).

  • Shape of the swim bladder: in ordinary characins, the rear chamber of the swim bladder is round or tube-shaped, but in this species the rear chamber is divided lengthwise into 2, joined at a single point at the top. The whole takes the shape of the letter π (［1］［2］).
  • Visible through the body: the muscles beside the swim bladder are very thin, and in live fish the 2 silvery, vertically elongated chambers can be seen from outside the body (［1］［2］). The 2016 paper writes that this swim bladder makes it immediately recognizable (［2］).
  • Colors in life (［2］): head and belly silvery. The body is semi-transparent, and the backbone and the boundaries between muscle blocks can be seen. Some fish have a spot of dark pigment in the middle of the base of the tail fin. The front tip of the anal fin is white.
  • Counted features: anal fin rays iii,45–48 (original description)［1］, iii,44–48 (redescription)［2］. Lateral line scales 33–38［1］, 32–39［2］.

Japanese sources also give as features the swim bladder seen vertically and shining (［12］［13］［11］), the black spot at the base of the tail fin (［11］［17］), and a diamond-shaped, deep body (［10］［11］［17］). These match the descriptions in the papers (inference).

Of the specimens examined in the 2016 paper, those from Peru were taken in Loreto Department (near Iquitos, the Napo River near Mazán, a stream near Pebas), Ucayali Department (a beach across from Pucallpa, a sandbank on the lower Ucayali River, a stream near Yarinacocha, and others), and the Ampiyacu River (［2］). There is also 1 specimen said to have been taken in the Araguaia River in Brazil, but it was taken in collecting for the aquarium trade, and its locality record is considered doubtful (［2］).

A Japanese online shop’s product page gives「マルドナード・グラステトラ」(Maldonado glass tetra) as another name (［17］). This may refer to the place name Puerto Maldonado in southern Peru (inference), but no source explaining the origin of the name was found. None of the Peruvian specimens examined in the 2016 paper are from that area (［2］).

Where it lives

  • The 2016 paper writes that it is often collected near the banks of rivers and streams, and is frequently found over sandy bottoms and in still backwaters (［2］). FishBase says the same (［4］).
  • The type specimens of the original description were collected in a pond connected to the Mamoré River (［1］).
  • Several of the Peruvian collecting sites listed in the 2016 paper are described as beaches or sandbanks (［2］).

Water temperature and chemistry — no measurements found

No record measuring the water temperature, pH or electrical conductivity of the habitat was found in the sources opened this time. A Japanese explanatory article (［16］) gives the habitat’s water as pH 5.5–6.8 and 24–28 °C, but it does not say what this is based on, and it gives the scientific name of a different fish, so it is not used on this page.

4. Breeding records compared

No breeding records have been found yet.

Not a single record of breeding in captivity was found, either abroad or in Japan. Not only are there no records of success, no records of failed attempts to get them to spawn have been found either.

What was searched

  • Searches were made by scientific name (Protocheirodon pi, Leptagoniates pi) and by trade name, in English, German and Japanese. Only introductions to keeping and records of arrivals were found.
  • On FishBase’s page for this species, the items for maturity, spawning, number of eggs and so on have only headings, with no content (［4］).
  • A Japanese online shop’s product page shows the “breeding” field as “―” (［17］).
  • A Japanese explanatory article (［16］) has a chapter describing breeding steps, but it is not a record of the author breeding the fish, and it gives the scientific name of a different fish, so it is not treated as a breeding record.

The differences between the sexes are not known either

  • The original description writes that there are no signs of sexual differences in the fins (［1］). The 2016 paper also states that no differences between the sexes were found (［2］).
  • The German importer wrote in 2025 that females seem to grow larger, but that this has not been confirmed by dissection (［8］).

Is it really “hard to breed”?

The article quoted at the start says that it is expensive because it is hard to breed (［9］). However, whether it is hard, or whether nobody has left a record of trying, cannot be told from the sources this time. Rather than “hard to breed”, “no breeding records” fits the records better (inference).

All the fish sold in Japan that carried a label were labeled “wild” (fish collected in the wild) (［20］［21］［24］). No record of bred fish being sold was found.

5. Keeping and breeding

The short answer

Lining up the records, the keys to keeping them are as follows (inference).

  1. They are timid right after introduction, and may take time to get used to food (［15］［18］)
  2. Watch out for changes in the water when introducing them, and for white spot disease (［11］［15］)
  3. They come to eat prepared food readily, as long as it fits in their mouths (［8］［10］［18］)
  4. They can be kept with other peaceful fish (［6］［8］［10］［17］)

Temperament and swimming

  • The German importer wrote in 2009 that it is very peaceful and somewhat timid, and should always be kept in a group (［6］). In 2025, it wrote that it is not a schooling fish in the strict sense, and that once settled it forms small territories which it defends against its own kind in a harmless way (［8］). The description has changed.
  • The blog of a Japanese magazine’s editorial department writes that they occasionally chase each other lightly within the species, but are peaceful (［10］).
  • A Japanese fish guide site writes that, being timid, they may not come out much for a while after introduction, and get used to the tank and swim around after about a week (［15］).
  • A Japanese shop writes that for a while after putting them in its display tank it fed them frozen food, and they did not come up to the surface to eat prepared food, but later they came to eat fine prepared food (August 31, 2026; ［18］).

Food

  • The German importer writes that they readily eat ordinary food of a size that fits in their mouths, and do not eat aquatic plants (［6］［8］).
  • The blog of a Japanese magazine’s editorial department writes that they eat everything from prepared food to frozen bloodworms (［10］).
  • Among the online shop buyers’ reviews, one (2012) says that they are light eaters, so care is needed with food (［17］; needs checking).

Hardiness and disease

  • Japanese sources write that despite its appearance it is hardy (［10］［11］［15］［17］［25］). Among the online shop buyers’ reviews, one (2009) says that not a single fish had died after more than half a year (［17］; needs checking).
  • On the other hand, some sources say to be careful about changes in water temperature when introducing them, and that they easily catch white spot disease (［11］［15］).

Lifespan

A Japanese article gives about 3 years (［11］), but it does not give the original record.

Breeding

Since there are no breeding records, this page cannot describe how to breed them (chapter 4). How to tell the sexes apart is not known either (［1］［2］).

Is it really “expensive”?: it costs about 1,500–4,000 yen per fish (table above). On the same shop’s blog, L-size neon tetras were 150 yen each (October 3, 2020) (［21］), so it can be said to be much more expensive than common small tetras (inference). However, a shop’s online store page writes that although it used to be very expensive, the price has settled (［20］). Comparing the prices of 2019 and 2026, it may look as though the price has risen, but the shops and the periods differ, so whether the price has gone up is not known (inference).

Wild or tank-born?

  • All the Japanese sales records seen this time that carried a label were labeled「ワイルド」(wild), “Wild” or “WILD” (［20］［21］［24］). No record of fish bred in Japan being sold was found.
  • Abroad too, no record was found of bred fish being on the market.

Checking the fish that arrive

  • As described in chapter 2, some sources give a different scientific name for the “Peru glass tetra”, and there are other trade names that sound similar (［5］［16］［17］［22］［23］).
  • Whether a swim bladder divided into 2 vertically elongated chambers can be seen inside the body is the reliable mark of this species (［1］［2］). It is a good idea to check whether the fish that arrive have this feature (inference).

Legality and regulation

This species is not listed by CITES (the Washington Convention), and, as far as was checked this time, it is not among the species whose import or keeping is regulated by Japanese law either. On the IUCN Red List it is listed as “Least Concern (LC)” (［3］［4］). However, it is not known where, or under which permits, each fish in the trade was collected and exported, so this page cannot state whether the trade in wild fish was legal. What is known and what is not known are written separately below.

What is known

  • CITES: searching the species database of the CITES Secretariat and others (Species+) for Protocheirodon (genus), Leptagoniates (genus) and Characidae (family) gave no records (checked November 18, 2026) (［29］). FishBase also shows “Not Evaluated” (［4］).
  • IUCN Red List: FishBase shows “Least Concern (LC)”, assessed on October 30, 2020 (［4］). The catalog of fish scientific names also gives “IUCN (2023): Least Concern” (［3］). The 2016 paper recommended LC, because the species is widely distributed and faces no major threats at many collecting sites (［2］). The IUCN page itself has not been opened (unverified).
  • Peru’s prohibited species: Peru’s regulation on Amazonian fisheries (Reglamento de Ordenamiento Pesquero de la Amazonía Peruana, D.S. N° 015-2009-PRODUCE) lists species whose trade as ornamental fish is prohibited. A 2022 guide made by the Peruvian Amazon Research Institute (IIAP) and WCS (the Wildlife Conservation Society) covers the 35 species currently listed in this regulation, but the characin family (Characidae) is not among their families, and this species was not included either (［35］).
  • Which country they come from: the German importer writes that it imported them from Peru (［6］［8］). Japanese sources also say “native to Peru” (［9］［11］［17］［20］). However, this species also occurs in Bolivia, Colombia and Brazil (［2］［3］).

What is not known

  • What permits and documents are needed in Peru to collect and export ornamental fish that are not prohibited species is unverified, because the legal texts have not been opened.
  • Whether the Peruvian government protects this species nationally has not been checked (unverified). The rules of Bolivia, Colombia and Brazil have not been checked either (unverified).
  • From which country, which exporter, and with what documents the fish sold in Japan were imported is not known, because it is not labeled (［17］［19］［20］).
  • So it is not known whether the wild fish in the trade were collected and exported legally.

Japanese regulations: date checked

Checked on 2026-11-18.

  • Invasive Alien Species Act: the Ministry of the Environment’s “List of Invasive Alien Species, etc.” and the list of regulated organisms (PDF) were checked. The only fish on them are some members of the orders Lepisosteiformes (gars), Cypriniformes, Siluriformes, Esociformes (pikes), Cyprinodontiformes and Perciformes; Characiformes (the group this species belongs to) were not listed as an Invasive Alien Species, an Uncategorized Alien Species, or an organism that requires a species identification certificate (［30］).
  • List of Alien Species That May Damage Ecosystems (not a legal regulation, but a list to call for caution): neither the scientific name of this species nor any characin was in the file listing fish (［31］).
  • Act on Welfare and Management of Animals (Specified Animals): on the Ministry of the Environment’s page, the Specified Animals are about 650 species of mammals, birds and reptiles, such as tigers, bears, crocodiles and mamushi (a pit viper) (［32］). The list of Specified Animals also covers only the classes Mammalia, Aves and Reptilia, and fish are not covered (［32］).
  • Act on Conservation of Endangered Species: in the text of the enforcement order (e-Gov law search), neither the names Protocheirodon and Leptagoniates nor the name of any characin was found (［33］).
  • Fisheries Resources Protection Act (import permits): on the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries’ page, the fish that need a permit to import are the salmon family (Salmonidae), common carp, the genus Carassius including goldfish, bighead carp, silver carp, black carp, grass carp, Nile tilapia and red sea bream (the list in force from June 9, 2026), and no characins were included (［34］).
  • Washington Convention (CITES): as above, this species is not listed (［29］).
  • Whether this species is covered by other rules (such as prefectural ordinances) has not been checked yet (unverified).

This page’s position

This page does not state whether the wild fish in the trade were legally collected and exported. As above, it sets out what is known and what is not known separately. Not being regulated under Japanese law does not mean that collecting and export in the country of origin were legal. Nor does being listed as “Least Concern (LC)” by the IUCN show that the collecting or export of any individual fish was legal.

7. What is still unknown

Below are the gaps that the sources collected this time could not fill, and the points marked “(unverified)” or “(needs checking)” in the text.

  1. Matching the name to the species: most sources that give a scientific name give P. pi, but some give “Characidae sp.” (［14］［15］), Phenacogaster pectinatus? (［17］) or Aphyocharax alburnus (［16］). No record was found of fish sold in Japan being checked by the shape of the swim bladder.
  2. Origin of the other name: that the “Maldonado” in「マルドナード・グラステトラ」(Maldonado glass tetra) (［17］) is a place name is only an inference. The identity of the trade names that sound similar (［22］［23］) has not been investigated either.
  3. When it became known in Japan: what “around 2003” (［10］［11］［13］［17］) is based on is not known. In Germany it was called a “new species” in 2009 (［6］).
  4. Water of the habitat: no measurements of water temperature, pH or electrical conductivity have been found.
  5. Size: the maximum of 3.1 cm on FishBase (［4］) is smaller than the maximum of 35.6 mm in the 2016 paper (［2］), so the two conflict. Whether the Japanese “5 cm” (［12］［13］［15］) is total length, and what it is based on, is not known either.
  6. Breeding: no record of breeding in captivity, and no record of failure, has been found. The differences between the sexes are not known either (［1］［2］［8］).
  7. Keeping temperature: what 20–28 °C (［11］［17］) and 25–27 °C (［14］［15］) are based on is not known.
  8. Lifespan: what the “about 3 years” (［11］) is based on is not known.
  9. Keeping impressions: the online shop reviews (［17］) are short posts and need checking.
  10. What is traded in Japan: all the Japanese sales seen this time that carried a label were wild. Whether fish bred in Japan are on the market is not known.
  11. IUCN assessment: the page has not been opened, and the text of the assessment is unverified. The year of the assessment differs between sources (2020［4］, 2023［3］).
  12. Rules in the countries of origin: the details of the permits needed in Peru to collect and export ornamental fish, and how Peru, Bolivia, Colombia and Brazil treat this species for protection, are unverified.
  13. Japanese regulations: prefectural ordinances are unverified.
  14. Sources that could not be opened: the IUCN Red List page, a paper on the fishes of sandy beaches of the Purus River in Brazil (cited by the 2016 paper as a source for the distribution; it could not be opened because loading did not finish), and product pages of Japanese shops that had disappeared because the fish were sold out.

If you keep the Peru glass tetra and record whether the swim bladder can be seen divided into 2 vertical chambers, the water temperature and chemistry, how many fish you keep, how many years they lived, and whether you saw spawning or differences between males and females, that will be very useful as a record that can be compared with others. In particular, not a single breeding record has been found yet.
