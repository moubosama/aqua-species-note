1. Introduction — how this page was made

Apistogramma elizabethae is a slender dwarf cichlid from the upper Rio Negro in Brazil, South America. Japanese articles call it “the queen of Apistos” (translated) (［20］［24］), and one shop page has the headline “the pinnacle of Apistos” (translated) (［27］). One explanation writes that the common view is that it is hard to keep and that, because “few are imported” (translated) of the fish collected in the wild, its price is on the high side (［24］).

This page checks how far that reputation of being “hard to keep and hard to obtain” matches the records. It goes back to the 1980 describing paper, lines up breeding records from abroad and from Japan, and collects dated prices.

This page was made by collecting and cross-checking the following kinds of information.

  • Papers and research catalogs: the 1980 describing paper (［1］), a catalog of fish scientific names (［2］), and a 1994 survey report (［3］; its main text was not opened)
  • Databases and trade articles: FishBase (［4］), Seriously Fish (［5］), and articles by a German ornamental fish importer and wholesaler (［6］〜［8］)
  • Breeding records and forums from abroad: an article and a Breeder’s Award entry from a US aquarium society (［9］［10］), an article by a Czech hobbyist (［11］), and posts on an Apistogramma forum (［12］〜［18］)
  • Keeping records in Japanese: hobbyists’ blogs (［19］〜［21］) and an answer on a Q&A site (［22］)
  • Articles, shop pages and prices: explanatory articles (［23］［24］), a shop’s blog and shop pages (［25］〜［27］), and auction results (［28］)
  • Regulatory records: Brazilian rules (［29］〜［32］), the CITES species database (［33］), and pages of the Ministry of the Environment, the Japanese law database and the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (［34］〜［37］)

There are 4 rules for reading this page (the meanings of terms are also collected in the glossary).

  1. Numbers have sources. A ［number］ corresponds to the reference list at the end; clicking it takes you to that reference.
  2. "(needs checking)" marks things such as hearsay or stories with no source given, which may have been misheard or miswritten.
  3. "(unverified)" marks things whose original source could not be opened and which could not be confirmed.
  4. “Data from a related species” means numbers for fish other than A. elizabethae. The author’s interpretations are marked “(inference)”.

And what is not known is written down as not known. Only 1 set of measurements of the habitat’s water was found, and when and where it was measured is not known. No record was found either that counts the days to hatching from the day the eggs were laid. The open questions are collected in “7. What is still unknown”.

2. Identity

The 1980 describing paper

This fish was described as a new species by the Swedish ichthyologist S. O. Kullander, in a long 1980 paper that revised the genus Apistogramma as a whole (［1］［2］). The original is in English, and the summary below is by the author (AI).

Current nameApistogramma elizabethae Kullander, 1980. A valid scientific name［2］
  • Origin of the name: it is named after Mrs. Elizabeth Cabot Cary Agassiz (1822–1907). She was the second wife of the naturalist Louis Agassiz, took part in the 1865–1866 expedition to the Amazon, and was the main writer of the book about that journey. The name also reflects its closeness to the similar A. agassizii (［1］). The German importer’s article gives the year of her death as 1902 (［8］), but the describing paper and Seriously Fish give 1907 (［1］［5］).
  • Features at description: in adult males, the membranes between the 2nd and 6th dorsal-fin spines are greatly extended. The tail fin is rounded in females and young fish, while in adult males its middle rays are extended. There is a spot on the side of the body, a spot at the second vertical bar, and a spot at the base of the pectoral fin; there are no stripes on the belly and no spot at the base of the tail fin (［1］).
  • How the size is written: the largest male is given as “39.8/56.7mm” (［1］). The first figure appears to be the standard length and the second the total length (the length including the tail fin) (inference).

Which group does it belong to?

The describing paper placed this species in the “agassizii group” together with A. agassizii and A. bitaeniata (［1］). The Czech hobbyist’s article also puts it in the same group as A. agassizii, A. bitaeniata, A. gephyra and others (［11］). On the other hand, a Japanese explanation calls it the 「ペルテンシスグループ」 (pertensis group) (［23］). Whether the grouping changed after the description has not been checked in the sources this time.

How to identify it

  • The German importer writes that a feature unique to this species is that, when frightened, the body loses its color and 2 oval spots appear side by side on the flank (［6］).
  • The same importer describes a case in which juveniles of about 1.5 cm imported from Brazil in 2018 showed these 2 spots, but turned out to be A. gephyra as they grew (［7］). It also writes that they are hard to tell apart as juveniles, and that other species may be mixed in (［6］［7］).
  • The male’s tail fin changes shape as it grows. It is said to be rounded in juveniles, a shape with extended upper and lower tips (lyretail) in young fish, and a pointed shape (lanceolate) in adults (［5］［23］).

Color differences by locality

  • Seriously Fish writes that fish from the Içana River have more blue on the head than fish from the Uaupés River, and that strongly red fish said to have been collected near the village of Tucana on the Uaupés River are sold as “Super Red”. It advises not mixing any of the forms in the aquarium (［5］).
  • A Japanese explanation lists the locality names 「イサナ」, 「ウアスペス」, 「トゥッカーノ」, 「ヤワウィーラ」 and 「サンガブリエル」 (read roughly as Içana, Uaupés, Tucano, Yawawira and São Gabriel) (［23］).
  • On the forum, there is an opinion that “São Gabriel” is not a collecting site but the name of the town where the facility that holds collected fish is located (［14］; needs checking).

3. Habitat

Records in the describing paper

The only localities in the describing paper are the 2 streams on the right bank of the Uaupés River (［1］). At the same sites, 3 other Apistogramma species were also collected: A. brevis, A. meinkeni and A. uaupesi (［1］).

The describing paper says nothing about the water at this species’ localities. It also writes, for the genus Apistogramma as a whole, that the water conditions at most collecting sites are not known (［1］).

The describing paper has one more clue, about the season. From the smallest fish, collected in December, it writes that they appear to have bred in October or early November (［1］).

Records of measured water

The only set of measured numbers found is in the Czech hobbyist’s article (［11］).

All numbers are from［11］. The article says that in all the places where the fish was found, the water is very acidic and soft, but it does not say who measured it, when, or where.

Seriously Fish says it lives in slow-flowing tributaries, backwaters and streams where fallen leaves collect (［5］). The German importer writes that in the wild it takes sand into its mouth and sifts out food particles while expelling the sand through its gills, and advises paying attention to the sand (［8］).

Some write on the forum that this species is a blackwater fish (a fish of water stained brown by tannins from fallen leaves) (［12］), but no primary record describing the water color at the type locality was found.

How wide the range is

The catalog of scientific names gives the range as the Uaupés River system (Brazil) (［2］). Seriously Fish says it also occurs in the neighboring Içana River (［5］).

On the forum, there is a post saying it has also been found in streams near São Gabriel da Cachoeira on the Rio Negro, in the Tiquié River (a tributary of the Uaupés River), at the mouth of the Içana River and elsewhere, and that its range may span more than 160 km from end to end (［15］). The post names the people who collected the fish, but gives no published source, so it needs checking. The German researcher U. Römer published a report on a study of this species in 1994 (［3］), but its main text could not be opened, so it is unverified.

What these records tell us

Small streams of fallen leaves and sand, with a pH of about 4–5 and very low electrical conductivity, appear to be this fish’s home (inference). However, the only measurement is 1 set of unknown origin, and how the water changes with the seasons is not known.

What the records show when lined up

  • Spawning is easy to achieve: none of the 10 records is of fish that did not spawn. A forum post also says that spawning is not hard if the water suits them (［14］; needs checking).
  • The hard part is the fry: failures are common — eggs disappearing (［13］), the mother eating the eggs (［10］［20］), and eggs vanishing before hatching (［19］). Whether the free-swimming fry grow up seems to be the biggest hurdle (inference). A Japanese hobbyist also writes that swimming free is “only the starting line” (translated) (［19］).
  • Number of eggs: usually about 60, about 100 from an older female (［11］), and 105 (［21］). Between Japanese F1 fish, about 15 and 25 (［19］).
  • A wide range of water: the tanks where they spawned ranged from pH 4.0–4.5 (［12］), pH 5.3 (［9］) and pH 5.4 (［13］; but the eggs did not hatch) to pH 6.0–6.5 (［10］). The Japanese hobbyist (Tokyo) gives around pH 4.0 for the main breeding tank (［19］).
  • Water temperature: about 26 °C (［10］), 25–26 °C (［11］［13］). The 89 °F (about 31.7 °C) in the US aquarium society article is much higher than the others (［9］). It is as in the original, but whether it is a miswriting is not known (inference).

The database says it lays its eggs on the ceiling of a cave, and that the female cares for the eggs and fry (［4］). Seriously Fish says the female becomes very aggressive in small tanks, so the male is sometimes moved out (［5］). In the records too, there are many cases of the male being moved out (［10］［19］).

On the forum there is an opinion that this species does not form pairs, and that 1 male spawns with several females (［16］; needs checking).

From hatching to swimming free

  • In the US Breeder’s Award entry, the fry swam free 3 days after the hatched fry were found (［10］).
  • In the Japanese hobbyist’s records, it was 8 days (［19］; August 2020) and 9 days (［19］; May–June 2020) from the spawning post to the free-swimming post. These are counted from post dates, and may differ a little from the actual days of spawning and swimming free (inference).
  • An answer on a Q&A site says the fry started eating around days 3–5 after hatching (［22］; needs checking).
  • No record has been found that measured how many days it took to hatch at what temperature.

Number of fry

  • In the US entry, over 40 hatched, and about 10 remained after 30 days (［10］).
  • For the Japanese hobbyist (Tokyo), about 40–50 swam free, and there were over 50 at 3 months (［19］). The same keeper’s spawns between F1 fish produced at most about 15 (［19］).
  • The Japanese hobbyist (blog) also describes a case with another pair in which the male chased away the fry and the female as soon as the fry swam free, leaving only 1 fry (post of April 2022, ［20］).

5. Keeping and breeding

The short answer

Lining up the records, the keys to keeping them are as follows (inference).

  1. Soft, acidic, stable water (［5］［8］［23］)
  2. A tank with plenty of hiding places, set up so the female can escape from the male (［11］［18］)
  3. Fine sand and fallen leaves (［5］［8］［11］)
  4. Do not overfeed (［11］［17］［19］)

Temperature guidelines range as widely as 22–29 °C, but the actual records are 26 °C in the habitat (［11］) and 25–26 °C in the tanks where they spawned (［10］［11］［13］). Keeping it at around 25–27 °C should be within range (inference).

Preparing the water

  • A Japanese explanation says that what matters is not the pH number itself but “how well” (translated) suitable water can be maintained (［23］). It also writes that if the water is changed without adjusting the pH and the pH rises, disease caused by Aeromonas (bacteria) tends to appear (［23］).
  • Seriously Fish says it needs water with almost no carbonate hardness and very low general hardness, and suggests using RO water and acidifying it with phosphoric acid or similar if needed (［5］).
  • The US records used rainwater (［9］), RO/DI water with akadama soil (［10］), and RO water with peat (［12］). The Japanese hobbyists used adsorbing soil, and found ways to use both soil and sand (［19］［20］).
  • On the forum there is also an opinion that with water below a TDS of about 20, there is little need to add acid (［13］; needs checking).

Water changes and water for the fry

Seriously Fish says the fry are very sensitive to changes in water quality for the first few weeks, so small amounts of food should be given several times a day, and the water should be changed regularly but not much at a time (［5］). On the forum too, fry are said to be sensitive to nitrogen compounds (such as ammonia and nitrate), and a post recommends checking the nitrate (［13］; needs checking).

The Japanese hobbyist (Tokyo) added no water for 3 weeks after the fry swam free, and after that only topped up once a week for what had evaporated (［19］). The US entry changed 20% weekly to every other week (［10］), and the US aquarium society article 30% twice a week (［9］). The methods differ, but what they seem to share is not changing the water suddenly (inference).

Food

  • In the wild it is said to eat mainly small invertebrates on the bottom (［5］). For keeping, live or frozen foods such as brine shrimp, daphnia and bloodworms are recommended (［5］).
  • The Czech hobbyist says food should be on the lean side, not too much, and that live plankton is good (［11］).
  • A forum post says that fry which ate a lot of brine shrimp nauplii soon died, while fry which ate a little survived (2006, ［17］; needs checking). The Japanese hobbyist (Tokyo) also writes that after dropping in a clump of tubifex worms, a male that gorged on them died the next day (post of January 2023, ［19］).
  • The fry’s first foods were microworms (［9］) and brine shrimp nauplii (［10］［19］). On the Q&A site there is also an answer saying that newly hatched fry are very small, so infusoria (microorganisms) are good (［22］; needs checking).

Tankmates and tank setup

  • There is a post about keeping them in a 300 L planted tank with other dwarf cichlids, pencilfish, tetras and Amano shrimp, where they spawned every 3 weeks but the eggs never hatched and the female kept being chased by the male (［18］). The person who answered named a tank layout that was too open and tankmates that target the eggs as the causes (［18］; needs checking).
  • The Czech hobbyist advises making hiding places with flowerpots and tubes and providing shaded spots. The hobbyist writes that pencilfish are good companions, but also that they go after eggs and fry near the cave (［11］).

Breeding: steps drawn from the records

Here is a method worth trying, based on the 10 records (inference).

  1. Put a pair (or 1 male and several females) in soft, acidic water, in a tank with plenty of hiding places (［5］［10］［11］)
  2. Condition them well with live food (［9］［12］). There is also a record of them spawning right away after the male and female were kept apart for about 9 days and then put back together (［21］)
  3. After spawning, move out the male and any other fish (［10］［13］［19］)
  4. Do not fiddle with the eggs too much (［19］)
  5. Once the fry swim free, give small amounts of food many times a day, and do not change the water suddenly (［5］［19］)
  6. When the female starts chasing the fry after about 1 month, move the female out (［19］)

Wild or captive-bred?

  • Shops and auctions have both fish labeled wild and fish labeled F1 or WF1 (offspring of wild parents) (［26］〜［28］).
  • In Germany there is a record of bred “Red Belly” being sold (2020, ［8］). Japanese hobbyists were also raising F1 and F2 (［19］［20］).
  • Whether the locality names shown (Uaupés, Içana, Negro and others) are really where the fish were collected is not known. There is also an opinion that “São Gabriel” is not a collecting site but the name of the town where a facility is located (［14］; needs checking).

Telling the sexes apart

  • Males are larger than females, more brightly colored, and have long, extended fins (［5］). The largest at description were a 39.8 mm male and a 23.8 mm female (standard length) (［1］).
  • Females are usually olive, turn bright yellow when courting or raising young, and their black spot on the side becomes prominent (［9］). A Japanese hobbyist writes that this species’ female breeding colors come in gradually toward the time the fry swim free, rather than at spawning (［19］).
  • The tail fin is rounded in females and young fish (［1］［5］). While they are small juveniles, the sexes are probably hard to tell apart (inference).

Cautions when introducing them

  • Juveniles may have other species mixed in (［6］［7］).
  • Mixing fish from different localities makes the locality forms impossible to tell apart (［5］［15］).
  • Change the water in a way that does not suddenly raise the pH of low-pH water (［23］).

Legality and regulation

The rules of the country of origin, Brazil, have changed greatly over time. Since it is not known when and where the fish in the trade were collected, it cannot be stated at this point whether the collection and export of wild fish was legal. What is known and what is not known are written separately below.

What is known

Brazil’s rules on the native freshwater fish that may be collected as ornamental fish changed as follows (［29］〜［32］).

  • The 2005 and 2008 rules said that native species not listed in Annex I must not be collected for ornamental purposes (［29］［30］).
  • The 2005 rule says it repeals IBAMA rules of 1992 and 1994 and rules of the state of Amazonas of 2002 and 2003 (［29］). On the other hand, no article repealing earlier rules was found in the texts of the 2008 and 2012 rules that were read (［30］［31］). Which rule was in force until when is unverified.
  • IN 202/2008 is the rule for marine and brackish-water fish; freshwater fish were covered by IN 203/2008, issued on the same day (［30］).
  • The 2020 rule repeals Annex I and other parts of the 2012 rule, and takes effect from May 1, 2020 (［32］).
  • CITES: a search of the CITES species database (Species+) for the genus name Apistogramma found no match (November 18, 2026, ［33］). The CITES field on FishBase says “Not Evaluated” (［4］).
  • IUCN: “Least Concern (LC)” on the Red List (assessment of November 7, 2018, as shown on FishBase［4］).
  • Captive-bred fish: there are records of bred fish being sold and raised in Germany and Japan (［8］［19］［20］［28］).

What is not known

  • 2005–2012: this species was not on the lists of species permitted for collection in the 2005 and 2008 rules (［29］［30］). When, where and through what procedures the wild fish traded in this period left the country is not known. There are forum posts about keeping them in 2006–2007 (［12］［17］), but they do not say whether the fish were wild.
  • Before 2005: whether this species could be handled under the IBAMA rules of 1992 and 1994 and the Amazonas state rules of 2002 and 2003 (all repealed by the 2005 rule) is unverified, because the rules could not be opened. The Czech hobbyist’s fish of 1993 (［11］) and the 1996 “first import” (［23］) fall in this period.
  • From May 2020: whether this species is on Brazil’s official list of threatened species is unverified, because the list could not be opened. Whether the 2020 rule is still in force, and the current rule on export procedures (one named Portaria IBAMA 102/2022 came up in a search), have not been opened and are unverified.
  • Land at the collecting sites: the author (AI) understands that much of the Uaupés and Içana river basins lies within Indigenous lands (Terra Indígena), but this was not checked in the sources this time (unverified). What permits are needed to collect fish there is not known either.
  • Countries other than Brazil: which country and place the locality name “San Felipe” in the US entry (［10］) refers to is not stated. If the fish were collected in a country other than Brazil, that country’s rules would apply, but this has not been checked.
  • Whether the fish sold in Japan are wild or captive-bred, and if wild, when and how they were exported, is not known, because the labels are not backed up (［25］〜［28］).
  • So it is not known whether the wild fish in the trade were legally collected and exported.

Japanese regulations: date checked

Checked on 2026-11-18.

  • Invasive Alien Species Act: the Ministry of the Environment’s “List of Invasive Alien Species, etc.” was checked. No fish of the family Cichlidae (the cichlids) was on the lists of fish designated as Invasive Alien Species or Uncategorized Alien Species (［34］).
  • List of alien species that damage ecosystems: the only cichlids on its list of fish were tilapias and the convict cichlid; Apistogramma was not listed (［35］).
  • Act on Conservation of Endangered Species: “アピスト” (Apisto) and “Apistogramma” were not found in its Enforcement Order (the version in force from March 5, 2026) (［36］).
  • Import permits under the Fisheries Resources Protection Act: the fish that require an import permit are the salmon family (Salmonidae), the common carp, the crucian carps of the genus Carassius such as the goldfish, the bighead carp, the silver carp, the black carp, the grass carp, the Nile tilapia and the red sea bream; this species was not among them (page updated June 9, 2026, ［37］).
  • CITES: as in “What is known” above, no match on Species+ (［33］).
  • Others (such as prefectural ordinances) have not been checked yet.

This page’s position

This page does not state whether the wild fish in the trade were legally collected and exported. It sets out what is known and what is not known, as above.

7. What is still unknown

Below are the gaps that the sources collected this time could not fill, and the points marked “(unverified)” or “(needs checking)” in the text.

  1. Habitat water: there is only 1 set of measurements (10 µS/cm, pH 4.7, 26 °C), and who measured it, when and where is not stated (［11］). The describing paper has no records of the water (［1］). Römer’s 1994 report (［3］) is unverified.
  2. How wide the range is: localities other than the Uaupés and Içana rivers (near São Gabriel, the Tiquié River and others) come only from a forum post and need checking (［15］).
  3. Grouping: the difference between the describing paper’s “agassizii group” (［1］) and the Japanese explanation’s “pertensis group” (［23］).
  4. Days to hatch: there is no record of the days of spawning and hatching together with the water temperature. The 8–9 days to swimming free are counted from blog post dates (［19］).
  5. Water temperature record: whether the 89 °F (about 31.7 °C) in the US aquarium society article is a miswriting (［9］).
  6. Claims about raising fry: fry dying from eating too much brine shrimp (［17］), the first 1–2 weeks being hard (［14］), and infusoria being good (［22］) all need checking.
  7. Relations between the sexes: the claim that it does not form pairs and that 1 male spawns with several females (［16］) needs checking.
  8. Lifespan and common diseases: apart from the explanation that Aeromonas disease tends to appear (［23］), no records were found.
  9. When it first came into Japan: the 1996 date (［23］) does not say whether it was the first import into Japan.
  10. Whether the fish in Japan are wild or captive-bred: the labels are not backed up (［25］〜［28］).
  11. Brazil’s rules of 1992–2005, and the list of threatened species and export rules from 2020: unverified. The relationship between the collecting sites and Indigenous lands is also unverified.
  12. Sources that could not be opened: the spawning post (February 2014) on the Japanese blog apistogramma.tokyo could not be reached, and the Rakuten product page for “EU bred” fish was not opened (unverified, November 18, 2026).

If you breed A. elizabethae and record the days of spawning, hatching and swimming free, the water temperature and water chemistry at those times, and the numbers of eggs and fry, that will be one of the few proper breeding records of this fish.
