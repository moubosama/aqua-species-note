1. Introduction — how this page was made

The Tucano tetra is a very small characin that does not reach 2 cm in body length even as an adult. Japanese articles introduce it as “a rare fish that lives in a very limited part of the Uaupés River basin” (translated) in Brazil (［13］), and one article writes, “There are tropical fish that are ranked as rare because few reach Japan and they are hard to breed.” (translated) (［14］). Yet after collecting the records, it turned out that neither “how rare is it?” nor “is its habitat really small?” is clear. This page checks what lies behind those claims.

This page was made by collecting and cross-checking the following kinds of information.

  • Papers and research databases: the 1997 describing paper (［1］; its main text could not be opened, and the bibliographic details were checked in［2］), a 2021 paper that examined the skeleton (［3］), and a catalog of fish scientific names (［2］)
  • Data sheets: Seriously Fish (［4］), FishBase (［5］), articles by a German importer (［6］［7］) and a UK fishkeeping site (［8］)
  • Breeding records from abroad: an article from a US aquarium society (［9］) and a forum post by a US hobbyist (［10］)
  • Records from Japan: a tropical fish shop owner’s blog post recording spawning (［11］), a keeper’s diary (［12］), and articles, shop pages and auction results (［13］〜［20］)

There are 4 rules for reading this page (the meanings of terms are also collected in the glossary).

  1. Numbers have sources. A ［number］ corresponds to the reference list at the end; clicking it takes you to that reference.
  2. "(needs checking)" marks things such as information picked up from auto-captions or hearsay, which may have been misheard or miswritten.
  3. "(unverified)" marks things whose original source could not be opened and which could not be confirmed.
  4. “Data from a related species” means numbers for fish other than the Tucano tetra. The author’s interpretations are marked “(inference)”.

And what is not known is written down as not known. No record could be found that measured the number of eggs, or at what temperature and after how many hours the eggs hatch. The open questions are collected in “7. What is still unknown”.

2. Identity

A small characin, the only species in its genus

In 1997, J. Géry and U. Römer described it as a new genus and a new species from the Uaupés River basin in Brazil (［1］［2］). The genus Tucanoichthys contains only this 1 species (［4］).

  • Origin of the name: it is named after the Tukano people, an Indigenous people living from the upper Rio Negro to the Uaupés River basin. ichthys is Greek for “fish” (［4］).
  • The specimen the name is based on (holotype): it is kept at the Museum of Zoology of the University of São Paulo, Brazil (［2］).

Which group does it belong to?

The two authors who described it did not place this fish in any subfamily of the family Characidae, and treated it as a “genus of uncertain placement” (as introduced in［3］). Later, a study using genes found that it is closest to the genus Tyttobrycon, another small characin (as introduced in［3］; the original study was not opened). The current catalog of fish scientific names and FishBase place it in the subfamily Tyttobryconinae of the family Acestrorhamphidae (［2］［5］).

What its small size means

This fish is studied as an example of “miniaturization”, in which the body stays very small even when mature. A 2021 paper examined the skeleton in detail and reported that 10 skeletal elements are entirely absent and that the bones around the lateral line of the head (the organ that senses water movement) are reduced (［3］). The lateral line on the body is also open on only a few scales (［4］).

Appearance and sexes

  • A broad black band runs along the side of the body (［4］). A Japanese article describes a yellowish transparent body with a broad black band and a red marking under the jaw (［13］).
  • Males are more strongly colored, with red in the dorsal, tail and anal fins. Mature females look rounder, especially when carrying eggs (［4］). The German importer also writes that the red in the male’s tail and anal fins is absent in females (［6］).
  • Juveniles are colored differently from the parents. Fry that have just started swimming have a glowing blue-green marking (［4］), and the US breeder writes that there is a glowing blue-green line just above the lateral line, which disappears within a few weeks as the adult colors come in (［9］).

3. Habitat

Known only from the Uaupés River system

This species is known only from the Uaupés River system in the upper Rio Negro, in the state of Amazonas, Brazil (［2］［4］). The type locality is a stream flowing into the Yavuari (Igarapé Yavuari), a tributary of the Uaupés River (［2］).

A Japanese article says “a very limited part of the basin” (translated) (［13］), but Seriously Fish writes that its full range is unclear and there may be other places where it lives (［4］). From the sources this time, it is not possible to tell whether it “lives only in a small area” or whether “few places have been surveyed” (inference). The IUCN Red List assesses it as “Least Concern (LC)” (assessment of November 7, 2018, as shown on FishBase［5］).

The water at the type locality

Seriously Fish gives the following conditions at the type locality in 1994 (［4］). This appears to be the record of the survey for the describing paper (inference), but the main text of the describing paper could not be opened, so the original record is unverified.

All numbers are from［4］. In the same place lived the small cichlid Ivanacara adoketa, a species of Crenicichla, a species of the small catfish genus Amblydoras, a species of Rivulus, and Poecilocharax weitzmani (［4］).

Some Japanese explanations describe the habitat as “a pond surrounded by forest” (translated) with pH 4.0 (［17］), but they do not give the original record. This conflicts with Seriously Fish’s description (a fast-flowing stream).

What these numbers tell us

Black water full of fallen leaves, with a pH around 4 and very low electrical conductivity, is this fish’s home. However, this is a record from only 1 place on 1 day, and how it changes with the seasons is not known.

Another thing that stands out is the speed of the current. Some Japanese articles say to adjust the flow to “a gentle current” (translated) (［14］) or that a weak current is better (［15］), but the type locality had a fast current (［4］). However, it is not known whether the current is also fast where the leaves pile up, and whether a strong current is needed in the aquarium is not known (inference).

What the records show when lined up

All 3 follow the same pattern: “a few fry survived, in the parents’ tank or in a tank the parents had been moved out of.” There is no record of getting large numbers.

  • Few per round: in the US aquarium society article, 8 were raised the first time and an average of about 10 each time after that, and the author also writes that this is few for a tetra (［9］). In the Japanese shop owner’s record, there was 1 fry (［11］).
  • Very soft, acidic water: the water in which they spawned was pH 5.0 and TDS 26 ppm (［9］) and pH 4.0–5.1 with conductivity below 50 (［10］), close to the type locality’s pH 4.09 and 24 µS/cm (［4］). The Japanese record has no numbers (［11］).
  • Temperature: about 21–24 °C (70–75 °F, no heater, ［9］). The other record says only “76–77”, with no unit (［10］). Since it is a US post, it is probably Fahrenheit (about 24–25 °C) (inference).
  • Fallen leaves and moss: all 3 were tanks with fallen leaves, moss (Java moss, willow moss) or floating plants (［9］［10］［11］).

The account that “the male guards the eggs” and the record that “the eggs are dropped one at a time and eaten by other fish” do not fit together. The sources this time cannot say which is usual.

Time to hatch

The data sheet says hatching takes about 24 hours (［4］). A Japanese explanation also says the male guards the eggs and they hatch in about 24 hours (［17］). However, no record has been found that measured how many hours it took at what temperature. In the US aquarium society article, 2 nights after the parents were moved, fry were hanging on the glass, and they started swimming the next day (［9］). Because it is not known when the eggs were laid, the time to hatch cannot be worked out.

Size of the fry

In the US aquarium society article, the fry were transparent when found and about 1/8 inch (about 3 mm) (［9］). The forum post gives “1–1.5 mm” (［10］), but whether this is a miswritten unit is not known.

5. Keeping and breeding

The short answer

Lining up the records, the keys to keeping them are as follows (inference).

  1. Put them in a well-established tank (one that has been running for some time) (［4］)
  2. Very soft, acidic water, fallen leaves and driftwood (［4］［9］［10］)
  3. Small live food that fits in their mouths, every day (［4］［9］)
  4. A group of 8–10 or more, in a tank that is not too bright (［4］)

Temperature guidelines range as widely as 20–28 °C depending on the source, but the actual records are 24–26 °C at the type locality (［4］) and about 21–25 °C in the tanks where they bred (［9］［10］). Keeping it at around 22–26 °C should be within range (inference).

Preparing the water

  • The US breeder used only rainwater, and added fallen leaves and driftwood to bring it to pH 5.0 (［9］).
  • Seriously Fish says breeding needs water with almost no carbonate hardness and very low general hardness, and suggests using RO water (near-pure water made with a reverse osmosis membrane) and acidifying it with phosphoric acid or similar if needed. It says peat need not be used, because harvesting it damages the environment (［4］).
  • A Japanese keeper kept them for over a month without losing a single fish, changing the water with water treated with a product that lowers KH (diary from March 2012, ［12］). The keeper worried “whether the pH swings are too large” (translated) (［12］).

Water changes: two opposite approaches

The US breeder changed about 50% every week, and the fish became more active each time (［9］). The other US hobbyist bred them in murky brown water that was changed at most once a month (［10］). Since fry grew up both ways, stable water may matter more than the amount changed (inference). Seriously Fish writes that they are sensitive to changes in how dirty the water is, and should never be put into a tank that is not established (［4］).

Food

  • In the wild they appear to eat tiny invertebrates and zooplankton (［4］).
  • They also eat dried food small enough to fit in their mouths, but the advice is to give small live or frozen foods every day, such as brine shrimp nauplii, daphnia, Moina and grindal worms (［4］).
  • The US breeder collected mosquito egg rafts outdoors, floated them on the surface, and fed the fish the larvae that hatched. Larger mosquito larvae and pupae were too big for them to eat (［9］).
  • A Japanese keeper fed brine shrimp (［12］). A Japanese article says that because they are so small, they often struggle at feeding time, and advises care in choosing tankmates (［14］).

Groups and tankmates

  • They live in groups but do not always swim together, and males often skirmish with each other. Kept in groups of 8–10 or more, they are said to be less timid and to show interesting behavior (［4］).
  • It is said to be best to keep them alone or only with small, peaceful characins and small catfish. Since they swim in the middle to upper layers, they are also said to suit tanks of small cichlids such as Apistogramma, as a fish that eases the other fish’s wariness (a dither fish) (［4］).
  • A Japanese keeper mainly kept Apistogramma and characins, and writes that the two cannot really be called a good match (comments on the 2012 diary, ［12］). Whether this species and Apistogramma were kept in the same tank is not stated.

Lighting and layout

  • Dim lighting suits them, and plants that grow under it are said to work: Microsorum, willow moss and its relatives, Cryptocoryne, and floating plants (Ceratopteris, such as water sprite) (［4］). The US hobbyist’s tank had no direct lighting (［10］).
  • With a sand bottom, driftwood and dried fallen leaves, the microorganisms that multiply as the leaves decompose are said to become food for the fry (［4］).

Breeding: steps drawn from the records

Here is a method worth trying, based on the 3 records (inference).

  1. Keep a group (8 or more) in a small, established tank with plenty of fallen leaves and moss (［4］［9］［10］)
  2. Make soft water of around pH 5 with rainwater or RO water (［4］［9］)
  3. Give small live food every day. In the US record, 2 females were visibly carrying eggs (［4］［9］)
  4. When a female’s belly suddenly becomes slim, assume she has spawned and move the parents to another tank (［9］). The eggs may not be found (［9］)
  5. The fry are too small to eat even brine shrimp nauplii, so provide microorganisms from moss and fallen leaves, and tiny organisms netted from pond water (［9］)
  6. Once they reach about 1 cm at about 3 weeks, they can eat brine shrimp nauplii (［9］)

As in the Japanese shop owner’s record, other fish may eat the falling eggs (［11］). To protect the eggs, ideas such as stretching a fine net under the floating plants or separating the parents early come to mind, but no record of trying them has been found (inference).

6. Before you buy

Is it really a rare fish?

Japanese articles call it “rare” and a “chinkara” (Japanese hobby slang for a rare characin) (［13］［14］［16］). Yet when dated sources are lined up, the picture is a little different.

Wild or captive-bred? Not labeled

None of the Japanese price records seen this time say whether the fish are wild or captive-bred (［18］［19］［20］). In Germany there is a record of captive-bred fish being sold (［7］), and in Japan there is an explanation saying that many bred fish are on the market (［17］), but where and by whom they were bred is not known. The specimens used in the skeleton paper were also obtained from the aquarium trade in Brazil and the US, and the paper says where they were collected is not known (［3］).

Telling the sexes apart

  • Males are more strongly colored, with red in the dorsal, tail and anal fins (［4］［6］)
  • Mature females look rounder, especially when carrying eggs (［4］［9］)
  • A Japanese article writes that when the fish are in good condition the yellow deepens and the tail fin turns red (［13］). It does not say whether this is a male-only feature.

Cautions when introducing them

  • Do not put them into a tank that is not established (［4］).
  • Their bodies are very small, so they tend to lose out when competing with other fish for food (［14］). If keeping them with other fish, it is safest to choose only peaceful fish with small mouths (inference).

Legality and regulation

It has not been confirmed how the rules of the country of origin, Brazil, applied to the fish in the trade, so it cannot be stated at this point whether the export of wild fish was legal. What is known and what is not known are written separately below.

What is known

  • The rule of 2012–2020 (IN 01/2012): it listed the native fish that could be collected and sold as ornamental fish (Annex I), and prohibited native species not on the list. This species was on that list (No. 724, ［21］).
  • The 2020 rule (IN 10/2020): it abolished Annex I above and changed to a system that permits species except those on the official list of threatened species, those in the CITES Appendices, and others (［22］; an unofficial English translation by a magazine).
  • CITES: a search of the CITES species database (Species+) for the genus name Tucanoichthys found no match (November 18, 2026, ［23］). The CITES field on FishBase says “Not Evaluated” (［5］).
  • IUCN: “Least Concern (LC)” on the Red List (2018 assessment, as shown on FishBase［5］).
  • Captive-bred fish: there is a record of captive-bred fish being sold in Germany (2015, ［7］).

What is not known

  • Whether this species is on Brazil’s official list of threatened species is unverified, because the list could not be opened.
  • Whether IN 10/2020 is still the framework in force, and whether this species is on the list of prohibited species published on the website of the Ministry of Agriculture and Livestock, are unverified (［22］). The current rules on export procedures have not been opened either.
  • Whether this species could be traded under the rules before 2012 is unverified (it had reached Germany by 2009［6］ and Japan by 2012 at the latest［12］).
  • A Japanese explanation says that the first collecting site was a stream used by Indigenous people, and that entering it required the chief’s permission (［15］; a story with no source given, so it needs checking). The relationship between the collecting site and Indigenous lands, and what permits are needed there, are not known.
  • Whether the fish sold in Japan are wild or captive-bred, and if wild, when and how they were exported, is not known, because it is not labeled (［18］［19］［20］).
  • So it is not known whether the wild fish in the trade were legally collected and exported.

Japanese regulations: date checked

Checked on 2026-11-18.

  • Invasive Alien Species Act: the Ministry of the Environment’s “List of Invasive Alien Species, etc.” and its list of regulated organisms (PDF) were checked. Neither this species nor any fish of the order Characiformes was listed as an Invasive Alien Species, an Uncategorized Alien Species, or an organism that requires a species identification certificate (［24］).
  • CITES: as in “What is known” above, no match on Species+ (［23］). The text of the CITES Appendices themselves has not been opened (unverified).
  • Other laws (the Act on Conservation of Endangered Species, the rules specifying fish that require an import permit, and others) have not been checked yet (unverified).

This page’s position

This page does not state whether the wild fish in the trade were legally collected and exported. It sets out what is known and what is not known, as above.

7. What is still unknown

Below are the gaps that the sources collected this time could not fill, and the points marked “(unverified)” or “(needs checking)” in the text.

  1. The main text of the describing paper: the 1997 describing paper (［1］) could not be opened. Where in the paper the maximum of 16.6 mm (as introduced in［3］) and the numbers for the water at the type locality (［4］) come from is unverified.
  2. How wide the range is: it is known only from the Uaupés River system, but its full range is unclear (［4］). It is not known whether “a very limited part of the basin” (［13］) is really small, or whether only a few places have been surveyed.
  3. Changes in the habitat’s water: the only measurements are from 1 place on 1 day in February 1994 (［4］). The water temperature and water level in the rainy season are not known. The original record behind the explanation of “a pond at pH 4.0” (［17］) is not known.
  4. How the eggs are laid: the account that the male stays near the eggs (［4］［17］) does not fit the record of eggs dropped one at a time and eaten by other fish (［11］).
  5. Number of eggs and time to hatch: there is no record of the eggs being counted. For “about 24 hours to hatch” (［4］［17］), no record has been found of the temperature at which it was observed.
  6. Size of the fry: about 3 mm (［9］) and “1–1.5 mm” (［10］) conflict.
  7. The unit of water temperature: the US hobbyist’s “76–77” (［10］) has no unit; taking it as Fahrenheit is an inference.
  8. Lifespan and common diseases: no records were found.
  9. When it first arrived in Japan and how much is traded now: the first arrival is not known. It seems to have been arriving regularly since the late 2010s (［17］［18］), but there are no statistics.
  10. Whether the fish in Japan are wild or captive-bred: not known, because it is not labeled (［18］［19］［20］).
  11. Brazil’s current rules, and Japanese regulations other than the Invasive Alien Species Act: unverified (［22］). The story about permission on Indigenous land (［15］) needs checking.
  12. Sources that could not be opened: the Aquatic Republic data sheet (a summary in the search results said there were no breeding reports) and the thread on this species on the overseas forum aquariumadvice refused automated access, and remain unverified (November 18, 2026).

If you breed the Tucano tetra and record the water temperature and water chemistry at spawning, the number of eggs and how they were laid, the time to hatch and the number of fry, that will be one of the very few breeding records of this fish in the world.
