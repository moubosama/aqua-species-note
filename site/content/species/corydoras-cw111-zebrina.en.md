## 1. Introduction — how this page was made {#intro}

Corydoras "Zebrina" is a fish that sells for tens of thousands of yen a pair, and wild fish can go for more than 100,000 yen. Yet little information is available in Japanese, and some shop descriptions come with a caveat that its breeding method "is presumed to be similar to other common Corydoras" (translated) (product page of a Japanese shop［28］; checked November 16, 2026).

This page was made by collecting and cross-checking the following kinds of information.

- **Papers and research-project records**: a 2022 taxonomic paper［1］ and explanations by Corydoras World［2］［3］
- **Breeding reports from abroad**: records left by keepers in Hong Kong, the UK and elsewhere, with dates and water parameters (Planet Catfish［4〜9］, the UK forum UKAPS［10］［11］)
- **Accounts from breeders and collectors**: a US breeder［14］, a UK hobbyist［15］ and a collector who knows the area［16］
- **Records from Japan**: the diary of a keeper who bred 1 pair in 2017–18［36］, arrival and price records left on shop blogs, keepers' videos, and auction results

There are 4 rules for reading this page (the meanings of terms are also collected in the [glossary](/aqua-species-note/en/glossary/)).

1. **Numbers have sources.** A ［number］ corresponds to the reference list at the end; clicking it takes you to that reference.
2. **"(needs checking)"** marks things such as numbers picked up from YouTube auto-captions, which may have been misheard or miswritten.
3. **"(unverified)"** marks things whose original source could not be opened and which could not be confirmed.
4. **"Data from a related species"** means numbers for fish other than Zebrina. The author's interpretations are marked "(inference)".

And **what is not known is written down as not known.** This fish does not even have a scientific name yet. Even for "at what temperature and after how many days do the eggs hatch", only 2 reliable records could be found in the whole world. I believe that writing this openly is the most useful thing for people buying an expensive fish. The open questions are collected in "7. What is still unknown".

## 2. Identity — name, classification and look-alike species {#identity}

### No scientific name yet: an "undescribed species"

As of November 2026, Zebrina has no formal scientific name.

In 2022, Brazilian researchers described 3 new Corydoras species from this region in a paper that also discussed CW111. But CW111 itself was not described, **because no typical adult specimens could be obtained**. The paper describes this fish as one of the most notorious species in the hobby (［1］). There are **only 9 specimens** in museums and similar collections, and most of them are juveniles (［2］).

### The names "CW111" and "Zebrina"

CW numbers are reference numbers that Corydoras World assigns to Corydoras that have no name. They took over after the C numbers of a German magazine stopped at C159 (［2］).

The first record is from a 2007 survey in Brazil, where it was given the provisional name *Corydoras* sp. "bonita" (［2］). Photos from the survey appeared online in 2008, and it became known to hobbyists around the world (［1］). When it first came into Japan, its arrival was also announced under the name "bonita" (blog of a Japanese shop［37］). Because another cory was already called "Bonita" in Japan, that name was not used, and "Zebrina" seems to have stuck (diary of a Japanese keeper［36］). In English it is also called the **Vulcan cory** (［4］［15］).

### What is "Hoplisoma"?

In 2024 the classification of Corydoras and their relatives was revised, and the databases ScotCat and Planet Catfish treat Zebrina as genus *Hoplisoma* (［12］［4］). However, the main text of the classification paper does not name Zebrina (CW111). The name CW111 appears only in the title of one of its references; putting it in *Hoplisoma* is the databases' treatment (［49］). This page writes it as "*Hoplisoma* (formerly *Corydoras*) sp. CW111".

### The most confusing one is CW146

- **CW146 (*C. thanatos*)**: a species described in 2022. **Its juveniles look just like Zebrina**, and even the extended dorsal fin of the males is the same, so there is a hypothesis that **the two may be the same species** (［3］). In Japan too, there are cases where fish bought as "Zebrina" later turned out to be CW146 (［36］). Some shops still sell it under names such as "New Zebrina" (［43］).
- **CW173 (*C. hypnos*)**: reportedly has fainter markings and a more elongated body (collector's explanation［16］). The paper that described it says it describes 3 species, CW146, CW172 and CW173, but the only one where it links a number to a name is thanatos = CW146; the number CW173 is not given in the section on hypnos (［1］).
- **CW171 and CW172** also live in the same area and are said to be hard to tell apart when young (［16］).

No reliable way has been found to tell Zebrina and CW146 apart in photos. "An extended dorsal fin" is a feature CW146 also has, so it **does not settle which species it is** (［3］).

## 3. Habitat — what is known so far {#habitat}

### The locality: "somewhere in the Serra do Cachimbo"

The fish comes from the area around the **Serra do Cachimbo** in the state of Pará, Brazil. But which river it comes from differs between sources.

| Theory | Details | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Upper Curuá River | Xingu River → Iriri River → upper Curuá River | ［4］［12］ |
| Jamanxim River | All known specimens are from the Jamanxim River, a tributary of the Tapajós. In autumn 2026, the label "from Jamanxim" also appeared on fish arriving in Japan | ［2］, ［43］ (a shop's label; there is no evidence for the locality) |
| Streams on the watershed | Small streams along highway BR-163, in the mountains that divide the Tapajós and Xingu systems | ［16］ |
{caption="Theories about the locality"}

The survey for the 2022 paper, at sites including the Curuá River, **found no fish that definitely matched Zebrina's pattern** (［1］). The exact location is **deliberately kept unpublished** to prevent poaching (［3］).

### What kind of water?

**Not a single actual measurement of the water in its habitat has been found.** The clues contradict each other.

- **Clear, soft water**: the forest is still there, the water is not warm, it is soft with a nearly neutral pH, and very clear (collector［16］)
- **A murky river**: the collection site shown in a Japanese magazine (2017) was a murky river (as introduced in［36］)
- **Blackwater**: the shop owner who imported it in 2015 said the river where it was collected had black water (hearsay in［36］, needs checking)

The related species CW146 has been collected mainly at night, from sandy beaches on the upper Jamanxim River to small tributaries, in places with **sunken branches, fallen leaves and sand** (［1］, **data from a related species**). Looking at the region as a whole, the Tapajós and the Xingu are classic "clearwater" rivers, with high transparency and a neutral to slightly acidic pH (［34］). None of these are measurements for Zebrina.

### The current situation of the habitat

The collector names deforestation, gold mining and large-scale agriculture as threats (［16］), and the paper records that tributaries of the Curuá River had been thoroughly fished with nets (［1］). This is one of the reasons to choose captive-bred fish.

## 4. Breeding records compared {#breeding}

The table above lists the breeding records whose dates or water conditions are known, one record per row. For the Hong Kong breeder's reports (［5］［6］) and the UK breeder's diary (［10］［11］), the original texts were opened on November 17, 2026 to check the numbers and descriptions carried into the table and the text.

### What the records show when lined up

Put together, the records suggest a pattern: **give a mature group plenty of live food and stimulate them with water changes in autumn and winter, and they lay about 20–40 eggs at a time, at a pace of weekly to every other day** (inference from［5］［10］［14］). The hard part is less getting them to spawn than **raising them all the way without weakening the parents or the fry** (inference from［14］ and other records).

- **Until the first spawning**: in every record of fish raised from captive-bred juveniles, the first spawning came at **about 10 months old** (［5］［10］［15］). With **young wild adults** arriving in autumn, the Japanese diary records spawning about 1 month after arrival (［36］). One shop writes that wild fish arriving in autumn grow out their dorsal fins in 1–2 months and enter the breeding season (［45］).
- **Conflicting numbers of parents**: for the Hong Kong group, the breeding report gives 8 males and 8 females, while the forum says 8 of 12 F1 were males, so the records do not agree (［5］［6］).
- **Other reports**: Planet Catfish has 3 more reports from 2023–25 (［7］〜［9］). One of them says the youngest parents were about 3 months old, with males at SL 50 mm and females at SL 60 mm; the report does say this, but it does not fit the other records and **needs checking** (［7］).

### Water at spawning: two opposite approaches

Here the Japanese and overseas records clearly split.

| | Hobbyist in Japan［36］ | Hong Kong breeder［5］ |
|---|---|---|
| Water color | Blackwater stained dark brown with fallen leaves | Water with tannins added |
| pH | 6 or below | 6.8–7.0 |
| Hardness | GH 0–1 (softened with zeolite and adsorbents) | No record |
| Temperature | No record at spawning (eggs at 23 °C) | 18.0–24.5 °C (no heater) |
| Water changes | 1/4–1/3 once every 5 days | Often spawned the day after a 90% change at night |
{caption="Water at spawning compared"}

The US breeder gets them to spawn every week in clear water without tannins (TDS about 125) (［14］). There is also a report that a Japanese auction seller keeps them at 24 °C, pH 6.2–6.7, with a water change once a week (［47］, hearsay).

Since they have spawned in both black water and clear water, **pH and hardness do not seem to be the deciding factor** (inference). On the other hand, there is a case where they did not spawn from April to September even with the temperature held at 23 °C, the pH lowered to 5.0 and more food (［47］). It is 1 example showing that **changing only the water chemistry out of season does not get them to spawn**.

What the records have in common are 3 things: **the winter season, frequent water changes and live food**. The hobbyist in Japan thinks the trigger may be the large change in water chemistry from water changes, rather than the change in water level (［36］, the keeper's own view).

### Season: autumn to spring in the Northern Hemisphere

| Record | Spawning period | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Hobbyist in Japan (indoors, air-conditioned) | Late December to April. Stopped when the tank was reset | ［36］ |
| Breeder in Japan | Female carrying eggs in December; pair in T-position in late May | ［48］ |
| Shop in Japan | June is not the breeding season. December is the breeding season | ［40］［46］ |
| Hobbyist in Japan (video) | Pre-spawning behavior in November | ［23］ |
| Hong Kong breeder | Males' dorsal fins in September, first spawning in October | ［6］ |
| UK breeder | Started in November, stopped in mid-December | ［10］［11］ |
| US breeder | Dorsal fins start to grow in April–May; late summer to December, January at the latest | ［14］ |
| 4 reports on Planet Catfish | October, December, March, October | ［4］ |
{caption="Spawning season"}

Overseas records center on October–January and Japanese records on December–May, so **in Japan the season extends into spring**. There are exceptions overseas too, such as a first spawning in March (［8］) and a spawning after days of rain at the end of May (［25］; the video description does not say where the uploader lives). The US breeder's account that the dorsal fins start to grow in spring conflicts with the Japanese records (they grow in autumn–winter and shrink in spring). Since most Japanese records are of wild fish that had just arrived in autumn, the fish may be bringing the rhythm of their home waters with them (inference).

In short, the time to aim for is **when the high summer temperatures are over and the water temperature is falling**. In Japan, that would be autumn to spring (inference).

In the Japanese diary, **at 48 hours the eyes could not yet be seen, and at 72 hours the black dots of the eyes were visible**; just before hatching, the eggs were rolling about (［36］). The Hong Kong breeder also writes that hatching them was not difficult (［6］). No source gives the diameter of the eggs.

## 5. Keeping and breeding — what the records suggest {#care}

### The short answer

Lining up the records, the key to keeping them is **an environment close to a "cool, clear stream"** (inference).

1. **A fairly strong current**
2. **Frequent water changes**
3. **A thin layer of sand, or no sand** (avoid deep sand)
4. **A calm environment where they cannot see people**

### Keeping conditions by source

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hong Kong breeder［5］ | 18.0–24.5 °C (no heater) | 6.8–7.0 | No record | Usually daily［6］ | Fine white sand, 1 cm |
| US breeder［14］ | No record | No record | TDS about 125 | 50% weekly | No record |

The data sheet for the related species CW146 gives a higher 24–28 °C (［13］), but this is **data from a related species**.

### Temperature and water chemistry

Among people actually breeding them, temperatures **mostly cluster around 22–25 °C** (inference). One Japanese keeper aims to keep it at 22 °C or above (［21］), and in another video sets it to 26 °C (［20］).

As for water chemistry, **they can be kept, and have spawned, in anything from black water at pH 6 or below to clear water around pH 7** ("4. Breeding records compared"). Some say soft water is not needed and that they do better with a higher GH (［19］, hearsay), while one breeder keeps them in almost pure RO water (［17］, needs checking). More than the numbers themselves, **keeping the water stable with frequent water changes** seems to be what matters (inference). Water at GH 0–1 takes effort to maintain, so for someone just starting, it is more realistic to **build a water-change routine with water you can keep stable** (inference).

Opinions also differ on tannins (substances released from fallen leaves and the like). The Hong Kong breeder adds them (［5］), and the US breeder does not (［14］). A Japanese keeper measured that fallen leaves lowered the pH and other values slightly, but puts more weight on their role as hiding places than on what they release into the water (［20］).

### Current and water changes

Many keepers create a **fairly strong current**. The Hong Kong breeder uses an external filter (1400 L per hour), 2 sponge filters and a powerhead (［5］), and the UK hobbyist says the fish dislike still water (［15］). Water changes, in the amounts that appear in the records, are **50–80% weekly** (［14］［15］［16］［19］). The collector gives as the reason that corys from clear streams are vulnerable to bacterial disease (［16］).

### Substrate, and color changes caused by the color of the bottom

- Planet Catfish says sand is almost essential and that sharp gravel should be avoided (［4］).
- The Hong Kong breeder laid **only 1 cm of fine white sand** (［5］).
- A Japanese keeper **lost several fish on deep sand** and switched to a tank with no sand (［21］).
- The owner of a specialist shop in Japan says it is easier to collect eggs from a bare tank (［18］, needs checking).

So it seems safest to **lay a thin layer of sand, about 1 cm, or no sand at all** (inference).

It has also been recorded that **body color changes with the color of the bottom**. In the Japanese diary, the fish turned darker on dark Oiso gravel, so the keeper switched to white sand (［36］). Another keeper writes the opposite: on white or clear bottoms the black faded, and only on black substrate did the color not wash out (［47］). So if you want to show a white body with bold stripes, choose a light bottom; if you want the black to look deep, choose a dark bottom (inference).

### Layout and lighting: keep people out of sight

- A Japanese keeper set up a low 60 cm tank (40 L) with no sand and **covered the surroundings with black boards so the fish could not see people**, after which the fish started eating (［21］).
- The Hong Kong breeder used no tank light, only the natural light in the room (［5］). A Japanese keeper also felt the fish moved about more with the light off (［47］).
- Some records mention leaves or shelters placed as hiding spots (［5］［20］).

In the records, tanks of 40–110 L are used, and in Japan, 45–60 cm tanks (［6］［15］［21］［22］).

### Food

- The basis is **sinking prepared food**, with **frozen or live foods** added (bloodworms, whiteworms, grindal worms, daphnia, tubifex) (［4］).
- The keeper who bred them in Japan scattered live tubifex worms on the sand so the fish could eat whenever they liked (［36］). Another keeper writes that daphnia got the best feeding response (［47］).
- **Their mouths are very small**, so choose food small enough to fit (［19］).

The US breeder had unexplained deaths the day after giving a new insect-based food, and stopped using it (［14］, 1 case). It is safer to try new foods a little at a time (inference).

### Groups, growth and tankmates

- The shop considers keeping **at least 6 fish** ideal (［28］).
- **Growth rates differ quite a lot between records.** In the UK, males were about 20 mm and females about 30 mm at about 10 months (［10］); a Japanese keeper's fish were about 3.5 cm at 8 months (［19］). On the other hand, among the F1 of the keeper who bred them in Japan, some were close to 5 cm at 10 months (［36］). The difference may come from whether they were fed heavily in their first few months (inference).
- They are very peaceful, and a species-only tank is recommended for breeding (［12］). There are cases of keeping them with cherry shrimp (［15］) and Neocaridina shrimp (minami-numa-ebi) (［22］). It is safer to keep them apart from similar species such as CW146 (inference).

### Getting them to spawn: live food and water changes

In almost every record, live food is used to condition the parents (Hong Kong: frozen bloodworms daily［5］; UK: blackworms and grindal worms twice a day［10］; US: brine shrimp, blackworms and microworms［14］; Japan: live tubifex worms［36］). A Dutch breeder's spawning video also has tags indicating live food (［24］). The Japanese keeper writes that live tubifex matters less as a trigger for spawning than **for growing the fish bigger** (［36］).

Conversely, there is also a record that **spawning stopped when live food was stopped** (［11］). This means that when they spawn more than you can handle, you can hold spawning back with the amount of food (［11］).

Spawning has been recorded as follows.

1. Males chase each other, shake their bodies and wave their dorsal fins in competition (［5］). However, they do not use their large dorsal fins for fighting (［23］)
2. Before spawning, the female **carefully cleans the underside of leaves** (［15］, needs checking)
3. The winning male follows a female carrying eggs, and they take up the **T-position** (［5］［36］)
4. The female swims around looking for a place to lay, holding the eggs in her pelvic fins (［15］［36］)

In the Hong Kong tank, **spawning took place between 12:00 and 15:00** (［5］). The eggs were laid all over the glass, on a green ornament (［5］), on the undersides of leaves (［15］), and in a row on Vallisneria leaves (［14］).

### Egg care

- **Telling fertile eggs apart**: freshly laid eggs are clear and cannot be told apart. **After about 12 hours, fertile eggs turn darkish and infertile eggs turn white**, so white eggs should be removed right away, it is said (［15］, needs checking).
- **Preventing fungus**: two methods are described: putting the eggs in a ring floating in the tank with plenty of aeration, or using methylene blue if moving them to a separate container (［15］). The Japanese keeper put the eggs in a plastic case floating in the parents' tank, added just a little methylene blue and kept them at **23 °C**, and no fungus appeared after 48 hours (［36］). In a German breeder's video, white fungus spreads from a single egg (［17］).
- **Do the parents eat the eggs?**: there is a record of fry appearing in the parents' tank (［11］), and the parents are also said not to hunt fry actively (［15］). However, in the Japanese diary the parents ate some of the first eggs (［36］). It is safer to collect eggs early once you find them (inference).
- **Fertilization rate**: in Hong Kong, 20 of 23 eggs hatched (about 87%, ［5］). The UK hobbyist's friend says about 30% with young males and about 50% once they are mature (［15］, needs checking). In the Japanese diary, the keeper later writes that the first eggs were fertile, but on the day they were laid also wrote that many seemed to be infertile, so the accounts do not agree (［36］).

### Raising fry: a small container, plenty of food, daily cleaning

**First food**: in the Japanese diary, the sac on the belly (yolk sac) was used up 3–4 days after hatching, and from then on the fry were given **rotifers, brine shrimp nauplii and powdered food dissolved in water** (［36］). Overseas, brine shrimp nauplii (［5］) and **microworms** (［14］［15］) are commonly used.

**Container**: in the Japanese diary, the eggs were hatched in **a small breeding box hung on the tank (a satellite)**, and the fry were **raised there for about the first month**, then moved to a 60 cm tank on day 40 with daily water changes (［36］). The keeper writes that the ideal is for **brine shrimp to be spread over the whole floor of the box**, that leftovers should be removed right away, and that using a large box for only a few fry scatters the food and wastes it (［36］).

**The US breeder's failures and fixes** (［14］):

- The fry were raised in a plastic container for about 1 week after hatching, and almost none died during that time
- But when they were moved to an **established tank**, they all died over time
- What worked was when **the tank, sand and sponge were all brand new**
- The newest method is **raising them in a mesh breeding box floating in the parents' tank and cleaning the bottom daily with a pipette**, and so far none have died

The Japanese satellite and the US floating mesh box share the same form: **a small container that the parents' tank water passes through, with dense feeding and daily cleaning**. That people in Japan and the US arrived at the same method separately is good reason to try it (inference). However, **neither left a figure for what percentage grew up**. In Japan too, there is a report of regular spawning but fry that do not grow up (［23］).

The same breeder says, from his experience with corys in general, that on a glass bottom the fins rot away from bacteria, so he **adds sand from around 9–10 days after hatching** (［14］). This is not specific to Zebrina.

These are the records of the F1 in the Japanese diary (［36］).

| After hatching | Size and appearance |
|---|---|

| 15 days | About 1.2 cm |
| 20 days | About 1.5 cm |

The figures for day 55 and day 60 are 0.8 cm or more apart only a few days apart, so **the diary contradicts itself**. They may be from different fish or tanks, or rough values judged by eye (inference). Here, please read them as a range: **"2–3 cm at 60 days"**. The diary also does not say whether this is total length (including the tail fin) or body length.

The time the pattern appears matches well with the UK record of "about 4 weeks after birth" (［11］). The adult pattern appears at about 4 cm (［16］).

### Common failures and fixes

| Failure | What happened | Clues for a fix |
|---|---|---|
| Deaths right after arrival | 2 fish died soon after purchase, showing red streaks (［10］) | Filter cleaning and daily water changes (［10］). Careful acclimation (［22］) |
| Wild fish weaken and all die | Spawned several times but never recovered, and died (［14］) | Choose captive-bred fish. Keep some F1 early (inference) |
| Deaths on deep sand | Several died in a tall tank with deep sand (［21］) | About 1 cm of sand, or no sand (［5］［21］) |
| Not eating | Frightened by the sight of people (［21］［20］) | Cover the surroundings, dim the light, provide hiding places (［21］) |
| Color fades or darkens | Body color changes to match the color of the bottom (［36］［47］) | Choose how light or dark the bottom is to match the color you want to show |
| Misjudging pairs by the dorsal fin | Outside the breeding season, males' dorsal fins shrink too (［14］［36］) | Also look at body shape and the pectoral and pelvic fins (［4］［36］). Check during the breeding season |
| No spawning | Too young (［17］) | For captive-bred juveniles, focus on growing them until about 10 months (［5］［10］) |
| No spawning out of season | Did not spawn from April to September even with changes to temperature, pH and food (［47］) | Aim for autumn to spring ("4. Breeding records compared") |
| Many infertile eggs | A report of about 30% fertilization with young males (［15］, needs checking) | Remove white eggs right away. Wait for the males to mature |
| Fungus on eggs | White fungus spreading from 1 egg (［17］) | Aeration, a little methylene blue (［15］［36］) |
| Fry slowly all die | All died after being moved to an established tank (［14］) | A brand-new tank, or a small container hung on or floating in the parents' tank, with daily cleaning (［14］［36］) |
| Slow fry growth | The food scatters and is not eaten (［36］) | Match the container to the number of fry so food reaches the whole floor (［36］) |
| Too many eggs to handle | Over 100 in 10 days (［11］) | Stop live food to hold back spawning (［11］) |
| Inbreeding | All fish from the same supplier (［11］) | Mix in another bloodline (［11］［15］) |
{caption="Common failures and fixes"}

Most failures are on the side of **"weakening and dying"** rather than "not spawning". The causes that come up are a high bacterial load, deep substrate and stress (inference).

## 6. Before you buy {#before-buying}

### History in Japan: first arrival probably in September 2015

Some articles and product pages say it was "first imported into Japan in 2014" (［26］［28］). However, the arrival that could be confirmed from dated records of the time is from **September 2015**: fish of 4.2 cm at 80,000 yen each (［37］). Another shop's blog also gives 2015 as the year of the first arrival (［44］). No record behind the 2014 claim has been found, so it is **unverified**.

Breeding in Japan also came early: by **October 2016**, "domestic" offspring of wild × wild parents were on sale in a shop (［38］). That is 4 years earlier than the oldest overseas breeding record found this time, the first spawning in Hong Kong (October 2020, ［5］).

The collector says that when exports were tight the price was over 1000 dollars, and in 2021 about 1/3 of that (［16］). Prices of Japan-bred fish in Japan have moved against that trend.

### 3 reasons to choose captive-bred fish

**Reason 1: wild fish weaken easily.** The US breeder got a wild group to spawn several times, but they never recovered and he lost them all, one by one. All he was able to keep were their F1 offspring (［14］).

**Reason 2: captive-bred fish take to prepared food more easily.** That is what the shop's description says (［28］).

**Reason 3: there are unresolved legal questions about where wild fish come from.** This is explained in the next section.

### Legality and regulation {#legal}

Sources start from different assumptions about the rules of the country of origin, Brazil, so **it cannot be stated at this point whether exporting wild fish is legal.** What is known and what is not known are written separately below.

#### What is known

- **What the researchers point out**: Corydoras World strongly questions where the fish in the trade come from, because Zebrina is not on the list of species permitted for export (［2］). This seems to assume the earlier system in which only permitted species could be exported (inference).
- **The 2020 rule change**: IN 10/2020 changed to a system in which any species that is not prohibited may be traded. However, for **species without a scientific name**, the condition is that reference specimens are registered at a research institution and published in Brazil's biodiversity database (SiBBr) (［32］).
- **Arrivals in Japan**: wild fish have come into Japan almost every year from 2015 until now (［37］［41］［43］). Even so, the legality is not clear.

#### What is not known

- Whether Zebrina specimens are published in SiBBr is **unverified** (［32］).
- Some sources say Portaria 17/2021 is the current framework, but its text and species list are **unverified** (［33］).
- So it is not known whether the export of the wild fish in the trade was legal.

#### Japanese regulations: date checked

Not checked yet (as of 2026-11-16). CITES and Japan's import regulations (including the Invasive Alien Species Act) have not been researched and are **unverified**. Once they are checked, the date checked and the results will be written here.

#### This page's position

> **Choose fish bred in Japan, and ask the shop "Are the parents wild or captive-bred?" and "What bloodline are they from?"**

### The bloodline may be narrow

The UK breeder worries that the fish in circulation come almost all from the same supplier, so the bloodline becomes too close, and writes that pairing F1 with F2 is better than pairing F2 with F2 (［10］［11］). The UK hobbyist recommends **bringing in fish from another group every few generations** (［15］). When buying, it is reassuring to get **several fish, from different bloodlines if possible** (inference).

### Pitfalls of sexing by the dorsal fin

Zebrina males are described as having a greatly extended dorsal fin (［26］［28］). However, **a male's dorsal fin is not extended all year round.**

- In the breeding season the dorsal fin grows to **2–2.5 times its original length**, and when the season ends it **shrinks back to about the size of an ordinary cory's**, then grows again the next season. This is not fin rot (US breeder［14］, UK hobbyist［15］; both taken from the captions).
- The Japanese diary records this shrinking with dates (［36］).

| Date (Japanese diary, wild male) | Dorsal fin |
|---|---|
| Late November (arrival) | Short. Not a broken-off shape |
| Late December | A few mm longer, slightly high-finned. First spawning around this time |
| January–February | Grows further |
| April 2 | At its longest |
| April 6 | A black spot appears at the tip and it stops growing. Spawning stops too |
| Late April to May 1 | Rather than breaking off, it **shrinks little by little from the tip over about 10 days** |
{caption="Changes in a male's dorsal fin (Japanese diary)"}

The keeper who wrote this diary says spawning began before the fin was fully grown, and that **far fewer eggs were laid after it was fully grown** (［36］). In other words, the length of the dorsal fin is not a sign that "now is the time to spawn" (a record of 1 pair). Also, in another keeper's tank, after one fish's dorsal fin grew, another fish's grew and the first one's shrank (［47］).

Whether what grows is **the whole fin including the spine (spiny ray)** (［36］) or **only the membrane** (［14］, captions) is something the records disagree on, and it **needs checking**.

So **judging "short dorsal fin = female" outside the breeding season can be wrong.** It is worth learning ways to tell the sexes apart other than the dorsal fin.

- Females are larger and thicker-bodied. **This is easiest to see from above** (［4］)
- Males have a lower body, sharper pectoral fins and pointed pelvic fins. Females have broad pectoral fins, rounded pelvic fins and a full chest and belly (［36］［4］)
- Some shops say **sex can be estimated from the shape of the dorsal fin even at around 3 cm**: close to an equilateral triangle means female, a tall isosceles triangle means male. Females have slightly shorter barbels (［39］). However, the same shop says in another post that sexing is difficult (［41］), and another shop says juveniles cannot be sexed (［28］). Treat it as **only an estimate**.
- Some people say fish sold as a "pair" turned out to be all males (［47］). Some shops state clearly that they do not guarantee sex (［42］).

### Risk of death right after arrival

- The UK breeder bought 6 F2 at 6 months old, and **2 died right away**. They had red streaks along the edges of the scales, and he thinks bacteria were the cause. Things stabilized with filter cleaning and daily water changes (［10］).
- The collector says corys from the clear streams of Cachimbo are **vulnerable to bacterial disease** (［16］).
- There is also a case where a fish with damaged fins and dull color got its pattern and sheen back in **about half a year** (［27］, 1 case).

The **acclimation procedure** published by a Japanese keeper is a useful reference (［22］).

1. Float the bag for about 30 minutes to match the temperature
2. Measure the pH of the water the fish arrived in
3. Add a little tank water about every 20 minutes. Repeat about 10 times
4. Record the pattern and fins in photos and video so individual fish can be told apart
5. Net the fish into the tank

This keeper's fish ate frozen bloodworms the next day (［22］).

## 7. What is still unknown {#unknowns}

Below are the gaps that the sources collected this time could not fill, and the points marked "(unverified)" or "(needs checking)" in the text.

1. **Scientific name and classification**: still undescribed. Whether it is the same species as CW146 is still at the hypothesis stage (［1］［3］). The main text of the 2024 classification paper does not name Zebrina; putting it in *Hoplisoma* is the databases' treatment (［49］). Whether CW173 corresponds to *C. hypnos* is also not stated by number in the main text of the describing paper (［1］).
2. **Exact locality and the water of the habitat**: the Curuá River theory and the Jamanxim River theory stand side by side, accounts of the water are split between "clear", "murky" and "black water", and there are no measurements (［2］［4］［16］［36］). The shop's "from Jamanxim" label (［43］) has no evidence for the locality, and the black-water account (［36］) is hearsay and needs checking.
3. **Egg size**: not in any source. Hatching is known from 2 cases (4–5 days at 23 °C, ［36］ / 5 days, ［5］), but the number of days at other temperatures is not known. The overseas shop's "3–5 days" (［35］) is unverified.
4. **Fry survival rate**: growth records were obtained from 1 diary (［36］), but nowhere is there a figure for what percentage grow up.
5. **Differences in growth rate**: it is not known whether the gap between about 2–3 cm at 10 months (［10］) and close to 5 cm (［36］) comes from how they were raised or how they were measured.
6. **Why fry die when moved to another tank**: the theory that bacteria are the cause remains an inference.
7. **Upper limit of egg numbers**: "80–140 per spawn, about 1000 a year" (［15］) and "300 in total" (［18］) need checking. The diary's figure of about 630 fry is an estimate worked back from the total (［36］).
8. **How the dorsal fin works**: whether what grows is the spine (spiny ray) or the membrane (［14］［36］) needs checking. Accounts from Japan and the US also conflict on the season in which it starts to grow.
9. **The reason for the seasonality**: why they spawn from autumn to spring, and how this relates to the rainy season in their home waters, is not known.
10. **Lifespan and common diseases**: apart from the red-streak symptoms (［10］), there are almost no records for Zebrina.
11. **Brazil's current rules and Japan's regulations**: unverified (［32］［33］).
12. **How to tell Zebrina from CW146 and CW173 in photos**: there are no reliable identification features.
13. **The bloodlines of Japan-bred fish** and **the basis for the claim of a first import in 2014**: neither is known.
14. **Numbers from captions or hearsay**: the water parameters, egg numbers and fertilization rate from the UK hobbyist's friend (［15］), the account of the specialist shop owner in Japan (［18］), the account of the breeder who keeps them in almost pure RO water (［17］), and the 2021 UK price (［15］) need checking. Among the Planet Catfish reports, "the youngest parents were about 3 months old" (［7］) also needs checking.
15. **Sources whose originals could not be opened**: the overseas shop's hatching time of "3–5 days" (［35］; the product page was gone), Brazil's rules (the SiBBr specimens and Portaria 17/2021, ［32］［33］), and the record behind the claim of a first import in 2014 could still not be opened on November 17, 2026, and remain unverified.

If you breed Zebrina and record **the water temperature at hatching, the days to hatch, the egg size and the number of fry that survive**, that will be valuable data even on a worldwide level.
