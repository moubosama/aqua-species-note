The Australian lungfish is a large lungfish that lives only in a few rivers of Queensland, Australia. In Japan it is called「ネオケラ」(Neokera) and is known as a very expensive fish. Japanese blogs and articles say that very few are imported, and that the fish available in Japan have a microchip inside (［12］〜［14］).

This page looks at whether those claims can be confirmed from the records, and in particular **which country's rules, and what kind of rules, the microchip comes from**.

This page was made by collecting and cross-checking the following kinds of information.

- **Catalogs and papers**: a catalog of fish scientific names (［1］), a paper on the history of the time when the scientific name was given (［2］), a database of Australian fishes (［3］), FishBase (［4］), a paper that estimated the age of a captive fish from its DNA (［5］), and records of a university research facility (［6］［7］)
- **Articles and forums from abroad**: English Wikipedia (［8］; for cross-checking), the page of an Australian native fish society (［9］), a reprint of a newspaper article introducing a fish farm (［10］), and a US forum for keepers of large fish (［11］)
- **Records from Japan**: articles and blogs (［12］〜［15］), a shop blog (［16］), and auction results (［17］)
- **Records of regulations**: the CITES species database (［18］), Queensland's fishing rules (［19］), Australia's threatened species database (［20］), Australian government declarations (［21］〜［24］), a state media statement (［25］), and pages of the Ministry of the Environment, the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries, Japan Customs and Japanese law (［26］〜［31］)

There are 4 rules for reading this page (the meanings of terms are also collected in the [glossary](/aqua-species-note/en/glossary/)).

1. **Numbers have sources.** A ［number］ corresponds to the reference list at the end; clicking it takes you to that reference.
2. **"(needs checking)"** marks things such as hearsay, forum posts, or articles that give no source, which may have been misheard or miswritten.
3. **"(unverified)"** marks things whose original source could not be opened and which could not be confirmed.
4. **"Data from a related species"** means numbers for fish other than *Neoceratodus forsteri* (not used on this page). The author's interpretations are marked "(inference)".

And **what is not known is written down as not known.** No record of breeding in Japan was found. No record that measured the water temperature or water chemistry of the habitat has been found in the sources opened this time either. The open questions are collected in "7. What is still unknown".

## 2. Identity {#identity}

### The 1870 description

This fish became known to science in 1870. G. Krefft, curator of the Australian Museum in Sydney, reported it in a letter to the newspaper *The Sydney Morning Herald* on January 18, 1870, and named it *Ceratodus forsteri* (［1］［2］). In the same year, a description also appeared in the journal of the Zoological Society of London (［1］).

- According to the history paper, Krefft did not want to wait months for publication in a scientific journal, and told the local newspaper first (［2］).
- The species name forsteri honors William Forster (1818–1882), a politician (Secretary for Lands) of the colony of New South Wales, who is said to have obtained the specimen (［2］).
- Krefft noticed that the teeth (tooth plates) of this fish resembled fossil fish teeth found in old rock layers (［2］).
- The catalog of fish scientific names says that the specimens the name is based on are not known (［1］).

- The parentheses in "(Krefft, 1870)" after the scientific name show that the genus has changed since the description. The history paper writes that it came to be called by the current genus name in 1982 (［2］).
- The other names given in 1871 and 1876 are both now treated as the same species (［1］).

### Names

- The history paper writes that the local Indigenous Gubbi Gubbi people called this fish "Dala", "barramunda" and other names. Krefft recorded the local name as Baramoonda and in other forms (［2］).
- Because its flesh was pink and tasty, the settlers called it Burnett River Salmon, after the river (［2］). The database of Australian fishes also gives this name (［3］).
- In Japan it is called「オーストラリアハイギョ」(Australian lungfish) (［15］),「ネオケラトドゥス」(Neoceratodus) (［14］［16］［17］), or「ネオケラ」(Neokera) for short (［12］).

### How to tell it from similar species

The other lungfishes are the African genus *Protopterus* and the South American genus *Lepidosiren*. A Japanese blog writes that this is the only lungfish listed by CITES (the Washington Convention) (［12］). That the genus *Protopterus* is not listed by CITES was checked on the [*Protopterus amphibius* page](/aqua-species-note/en/species/protopterus-amphibius/). Differences in body structure were not looked into this time.

## 3. Habitat {#habitat}

### Distribution

| Category | Rivers and places | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Native | The Burnett and Mary river systems | ［3］［4］ |
| Possibly native | The Brisbane River, the North Pine River | ［3］ |
| Moved there by people | The Brisbane, Albert, Coomera and Stanley rivers, Enoggera Reservoir | ［3］ |
{caption="Descriptions of the distribution"}

- FishBase says it lives in the Burnett and Mary river systems of southern Queensland, at 24–26° S (［4］).
- The Brisbane River appears both as "possibly native" and as "moved there". English Wikipedia writes that it was also moved into the Pine, Caboolture and Condamine rivers, but that whether it still survives there is not known (［8］).

### Where it lives

- The database of Australian fishes says it lives in deep pools, reservoirs and broad, slow-flowing stretches of rivers, at depths of 1.5–3 m (［3］).
- It spawns among thickets of water plants and roots, in flowing places with clear, oxygen-rich water (［3］).

### Water temperature and chemistry — no measurements found

No record that measured the water temperature, pH or electrical conductivity of the habitat was found in the sources opened this time. FishBase's 22–28 °C (［4］) does not say where it was measured. The Australian native fish society says it spawns in the wild when the water is around 20–23 °C, at a depth of about 1 m (［9］).

### Threats

The database of Australian fishes gives agriculture and forestry, introduced plants and fish, and changes in flow caused by dams and weirs as the main threats (［3］).

## 4. Breeding records compared {#breeding}

The table above shows the records from captivity (fish farms and a research facility). None of them is a primary record that measured water temperature or egg counts.

- **The fish farm record** (［10］) is a 2008 newspaper article reprinted by another industry website. It says the farm first bred them in 2001 and had succeeded 4 times as of the article. The egg count (15,000 from one large female) is the owner's word, and the original newspaper article has not been opened (**unverified**).
- **The university research facility** (［6］［7］) is recorded as having built a captive breeding group so that embryos and larvae could be used for research. In which years and how many times they spawned is not stated in the sources this time.
- **The government declarations** (［21］〜［24］) are the conditions of permits to sell or export bred young. Since the declarations exist, it is certain that fish are being bred in Queensland from parents taken from rivers (inference; the declarations set the condition that "only young of the first generation onward may be exported").

### Conflicting egg counts

Egg counts differ widely between sources.

- In the wild, 50–100 at a time (［3］).
- English Wikipedia, citing a book (2004), writes that in captivity 200–600 are laid at a time, and that a wild female lays at most a few hundred in her lifetime (［8］; the original book has not been opened, so **unverified**).
- A Japanese article gives 1,000–2,000, but gives no source (［12］; **needs checking**).
- The fish farm owner says 15,000 from one large female (［10］; **unverified**).

"The number laid at one time" and "the number laid in a lifetime", and "wild" and "fish farm", are mixed together, so the numbers cannot be compared as they are (inference).

### Records in Japan

There is a 2020 record that it had been shown at at least 9 aquariums and zoos in Japan (［15］). However, no record was found of it spawning or breeding in a Japanese aquarium or a private tank.

## 5. Keeping and breeding {#care}

### The short answer

Lining up the records, the keys to keeping them are as follows (inference).

1. **A very large tank** (adults are about 1 m total length; ［4］)
2. **Be ready to keep it for decades** (there is a captive fish said to have lived past 100; see "Lifespan" below)
3. **It grows slowly** (sources differ widely; see "Size and growth" below)

### Keeping conditions by source

| Source | Temperature | Water chemistry | Tank | Other |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FishBase［4］ | 22–28 °C | No record | No record | Does not say where the numbers were measured |
| Japanese blog［12］ | 24–28 °C | pH 6.0–7.5 | At least 180 × 90 × 45 cm. Juveniles smaller than 15 cm can start in a 45–60 cm tank | The weight of a large tank may mean the floor needs reinforcing |
| Japanese blog［13］ | No record | No record | A 60 cm tank for young fish, a 200 cm class tank once grown | The author writes that they have never kept one |
| Australian native fish society［9］ | No record | No record | No record | An easy-to-keep, gentle fish. But because of the price and the legal rules, it is for serious hobbyists |
{caption="Keeping conditions by source"}

As there are no measurements from the habitat (chapter 3), the water temperature and chemistry are guidelines of the "people kept it successfully like this" kind (inference).

### Size and growth

- The maximum is 180 cm total length (［3］), or 170 cm and 40.0 kg (［4］). English Wikipedia gives figures of 150 cm and 43 kg from an old book (［8］).
- The 2 fish seized in Queensland in 2020 were 79 cm and 3.22 kg, and 72 cm and 2.28 kg (［25］).
- Growth rates differ widely between sources. The society's page says it may reach only 120 mm in 2 years (［9］). The fish farm owner says it reaches 30 cm in 12 months (［10］; **unverified**).

### Food

- Larvae are said to eat newly hatched brine shrimp, daphnia, midge larvae and tubifex worms (［9］).
- The fish farm says it feeds the young fish algae and blackworms (［10］; **unverified**).
- The 2 fish the state donated to an aquarium were announced to be fed bananas and algae tablets (a sinking food) (［25］).

### Lifespan

The paper that estimated age from DNA notes that its method's yardstick has only been confirmed up to 77 years, so estimates above that are less certain (［5］). It also writes that the highest ages in captivity may have been extended by attentive care (［5］).

### Breeding

There is no record of breeding in Japan, so no steps can be given. The records from the wild (chapter 4) suggest that flowing places with thick water plants, and the seasonal changes of August–December, may be clues (inference). However, simply gathering fish large enough to be parents in Japan is thought to be difficult (inference).

## 6. Before you buy {#before-buying}

### Since when has it been coming into Japan?

- A conversation on the blog of an aquarium magazine publisher writes that it first came into Japan in the early 2000s, and that the Australian fish farmer held a press conference (［14］).
- The 2008 article introducing the fish farm writes that Japan is the biggest buyer (［10］).
- A Japanese blog writes that when imports began it cost more than 2,000,000 yen, and that imports stopped for a time (［12］).

Since the fish farm first bred them in 2001 (［10］), the fish that came into Japan are thought to be farmed fish from after that (inference).

### Labels of origin

- At auction, there were fish labeled「パインリバー」(Pine River) and「ブリスベンリバー」(Brisbane River) (［17］).
- A 2017 US forum post writes that the first bred fish to come into the US from the aquaculture company were bred from the "Pine River population" (［11］; **needs checking**).
- Japanese labels such as「パインリバー」(Pine River) may also refer to the river of the broodstock fish (inference). Whether they mean wild-caught fish cannot be told from the label (**needs checking**).

### Where does the microchip rule come from?

Japanese blogs write that fish exported officially with the approval of the Australian government have a microchip in the back of the neck (［12］), and that, as with arowanas, they have an identification chip and come with a certificate (［13］).

Looking into it, **the rule of inserting a microchip (PIT tag) is a condition of Australian government declarations made under Australian law**.

- Under the Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act (EPBC Act), the Australian government issues declarations making aquaculture businesses that handle this species "approved wildlife trade operations" (［21］〜［24］). It is thought that only businesses approved in this way can export bred fish (inference).
- The May 2020 declaration for the aquaculture company sets as conditions that every fish exported has a PIT tag (or another mark approved by the government) inside its body, and that the tag numbers and sizes are given in the annual report (［23］).
- The April 2024 declaration for the small hatchery sets as conditions that every fish has a PIT tag inserted before it is sold or transferred, and that for each sale the buyer, the tag number, the average length and weight, and the generation are reported to the government (［24］).

As far as was checked this time, no rule in Japanese law requiring a microchip for this fish was found (see "Japanese regulations: date checked" below). The Asian arowana that the blogs compare it with is a "National Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (international)" under Japan's Act on Conservation of Endangered Species, and there is a system for registering each fish (［27］［28］). This species is not included. **The "chip" talked about in Japan is thought to come from the export conditions on the Australian side** (inference).

### Telling the sexes apart

No record describing how to tell the sexes apart in this species was found this time.

### Cautions when introducing them

- The 2018 shop fish is described as having had golden mottling all over its body when it arrived, but now being the usual color (［16］). The color of young fish may change as they grow (inference).
- On Yahoo! Auctions there were also fish described as having damaged fins or clouded eyes (［17］).

### Legality and regulation {#legal}

This species is listed in CITES (the Washington Convention) Appendix II and is strongly protected under Australian law. In Japan, as far as was checked this time, it was not among the species whose import or keeping is regulated by the Invasive Alien Species Act or the Act on Conservation of Endangered Species. However, **under which permits each fish sold in Japan was bred and exported cannot be confirmed from the label alone, and this page cannot state whether it is legal.** What is known and what is not known are written separately below.

#### What is known

- **CITES**: listed in Appendix II, effective from July 1, 1975 (checked on Species+ on November 18, 2026) (［18］). Species in Appendix II can be traded commercially, but need an export permit from the exporting country (［31］). English Wikipedia writes that it was listed in 1977 (［8］), which conflicts. This page takes the Species+ date. Under EU regulations it is in Annex B (［18］).
- **IUCN Red List**: "Endangered (EN)", assessed on February 13, 2019 (as shown by FishBase; ［4］). The IUCN page itself could not be opened (**unverified**).
- **Australian federal law (EPBC Act)**: listed as "Vulnerable" (［3］). The listing is said to have taken effect on August 6, 2003 (［20］; the page could not be opened and only the search result was seen, so **unverified**).
- **Queensland's fishing rules**: under the state's freshwater fishing rules, both the size limit and the possession limit for this species are "No take" (page updated March 27, 2026) (［19］). The database of Australian fishes also says it is a "no-take species" under the state fisheries law (［3］).
- **Export conditions**: only government-approved operations can export bred fish. The conditions of the declarations include that exported fish are young (first generation onward) of parents taken from rivers under a state permit, and that they have PIT tags (［23］［24］). The 2024 declaration states that fish taken from rivers under a state permit cannot be exported by this operation (［24］).
- **Taking broodstock fish**: the aquaculture company in October–November 2016 (［21］) and the small hatchery from April 2015 (［22］) were allowed by the government to take broodstock fish from rivers under state permits.
- **An example of a violation in Australia**: in October 2020, state fisheries officers and police found 2 fish being kept without a permit in a garden pond at a house in Gympie. After investigation, the resident was said to have also held eggs without a permit, and was fined 5,063 dollars. The 2 fish were donated to an aquarium in December 2021 (［25］).
- **Import into Japan**: when importing an Appendix II animal, documents such as an export permit issued by the authorities of the exporting country are presented to customs for checking (［31］).

#### What is not known

- Whether the "certificates" of fish sold in Japan (［13］［16］) are copies of Australian export permits, or documents made by the shop or importer, is not known.
- Whether there is any way for a buyer to check that the chip number of a fish sold in Japan matches a number reported to the Australian government is not known (**unverified**).
- Whether the declaration for the aquaculture company that expired in April 2023 (［23］) was renewed afterwards has not been checked (**unverified**).
- How it is treated under Queensland's nature conservation law is described differently by different sources, and has not been checked (**unverified**).
- The text of any provision by which the government bans all export of wild fish has not been opened this time. The declaration states that "fish taken from rivers cannot be exported by this operation" (［24］).
- A Japanese blog writes that in 2019 a shop employee in Japan was arrested on suspicion of smuggling the fish by passing it off as another ornamental fish (［12］), but no news report was found this time (**needs checking**).
- A Japanese blog writes that keeping it needs a prefectural permit (［13］), but fish are not covered by the national Specified Animals rules (［29］), and no basis for this was found (**needs checking**). Prefectural ordinances have not been checked (**unverified**).
- So whether each fish traded in Japan was legally bred, exported and imported cannot be told from the label or from the fact that "it has a chip" alone.

#### Japanese regulations: date checked

Checked on 2026-11-18.

- **Invasive Alien Species Act**: the Ministry of the Environment's "List of Invasive Alien Species, etc." was checked. The only fish on it are some members of the orders Lepisosteiformes (gars), Cypriniformes, Siluriformes, Esociformes (pikes), Cyprinodontiformes and Perciformes; lungfishes were not listed as an Invasive Alien Species, an Uncategorized Alien Species, or an organism that requires a species identification certificate (［26］).
- **Act on Conservation of Endangered Species**: the Ministry of the Environment explains that the "National Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (international)" are species in CITES Appendix I and species under treaties such as those on migratory birds (［27］). This species is in Appendix II (［18］). Searching the text of the enforcement order on e-Gov law search for「ケラトドゥス」(Ceratodus),「ハイギョ」(lungfish) and "Neoceratodus" gave 0 results. 「アジアアロワナ」(Asian arowana), searched for comparison, did appear in the enforcement order (［28］). So the registration system (and the identification of each fish that goes with it) is thought not to apply to this species (inference).
- **Act on Welfare and Management of Animals (Specified Animals)**: the list of Specified Animals covers only the classes Mammalia, Aves and Reptilia, and **fish are not covered** (［29］).
- **Fisheries Resources Protection Act**: the fish that need a permit to import are the salmon family (Salmonidae), carp, the crucian carp genus including goldfish, black carp, silver carp, bighead carp, grass carp, Nile tilapia and red sea bream (updated June 9, 2026), and this species is not included (［30］).
- **Washington Convention (CITES)**: as it is in Appendix II, documents such as an export permit from the exporting country are presented to customs when importing (［18］［31］).
- Whether this species is covered by **other rules** (such as prefectural ordinances) has not been checked yet (**unverified**).

#### This page's position

> **This page does not state whether the fish in the trade were legally bred, exported and imported.** As above, it sets out what is known and what is not known separately. Not being regulated under Japanese law, or having a chip or a certificate, does not by itself mean that the fish was bred and exported in line with Australian rules.

## 7. What is still unknown {#unknowns}

Below are the gaps that the sources collected this time could not fill, and the points marked "(unverified)" or "(needs checking)" in the text.

1. **Water temperature and chemistry of the habitat**: no measurements have been found. What FishBase's 22–28 °C (［4］) is based on is not known either.
2. **Egg counts**: 50–100 (［3］), 200–600 (［8］; **unverified**), 1,000–2,000 (［12］; **needs checking**) and 15,000 (［10］; **unverified**) differ widely.
3. **Fish farm records**: the original 2008 newspaper article (the source of the reprint［10］) has not been opened (**unverified**). When and how much the fish bred at the university research facility is not known either (［6］［7］).
4. **Breeding in Japan**: no record has been found, either at aquariums or by individuals.
5. **Growth rate**: 120 mm in 2 years (［9］) and 30 cm in 12 months (［10］; **unverified**) do not agree.
6. **Telling the sexes apart**: no record has been found.
7. **History of imports**: there is no original record of the year it first came into Japan (the early 2000s; ［14］) or of its price then (more than 2,000,000 yen; ［12］; **needs checking**). A search turned up an old newspaper article saying that in 1974 the state fisheries department promised to send a male to a Tokyo zoo, but its text has not been read (**unverified**).
8. **「1匹100万円以上」(over 1,000,000 yen per fish)** (［13］): where this comes from is not known (**needs checking**).
9. **Labels of origin**: what the labels「パインリバー」(Pine River) and「ブリスベンリバー」(Brisbane River) (［17］) refer to is not known (**needs checking**).
10. **Certificates and chips**: what the "certificates" in Japan are, and whether there is a way to check chip numbers, is not known.
11. **Australian regulations**: the date of the EPBC Act listing (［20］), the IUCN assessment page, its treatment under the state nature conservation law, and the aquaculture company's declarations after 2023 are **unverified**.
12. **Japanese regulations**: prefectural ordinances are **unverified**. The story of the smuggling arrest in Japan (［12］) **needs checking**.
13. **Sources that could not be opened**: the draft national recovery plan (2017) and the latest documents, this species' page in the threatened species database, the IUCN page, a 1928 newspaper article (about a researcher who raised larvae), and an article in an English-language aquarium magazine (2022).

If you keep a Neoceratodus and record **when you got it and its size then, its size now, the water temperature and chemistry, its food, and what is written on its certificate (with numbers and the like hidden)**, that will be very useful as a record that can be compared with others.
