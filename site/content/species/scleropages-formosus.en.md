The Asian arowana is a large, long-bodied fish of the rivers and lakes of Southeast Asia. Its large scales and single pair of barbels stand out (［4］). A 2003 paper writes that this fish has become a very popular and very expensive ornamental fish in many Asian countries (［1］). In Japan it is sold under names such as 紅龍 (Super Red) and 過背金龍 (kahai kinryū), and among shop listing prices there were fish at 2,000,000 yen each (November 2026, ［14］).

On the other hand, this fish is listed in Appendix I of the convention on international trade (CITES, the Washington Convention) (［16］), and in Japan it is designated a「国際希少野生動植物種」(Internationally Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora) under 種の保存法 (Act on Conservation of Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora) (［18］). Buying and selling it requires a「登録票」(registration card) issued by the Japan Wildlife Research Center (［19］［21］). This page checks "why is it so expensive?", "is it really rare?" and "how can you keep it while following the rules?" against the records.

This page was made by collecting and cross-checking the following kinds of information.

- **Papers and research databases**: the 2003 paper that split the color forms into separate species (［1］), a catalog of fish scientific names (［2］), a 1976 paper on breeding in the wild in Malaysia (［3］; only the abstract was read), FishBase (［4］), and a database of the origins of scientific names (［5］)
- **Data sheets and articles from abroad**: Seriously Fish (［6］), a UK magazine's article on a farm visit (［7］), a Malaysian research institute's breeding record (［8］; read in a reprint), a New Zealand zoo's breeding report (［9］), and a news report on an exhibition in Indonesia (［10］)

- **Price information**: shop listing prices (［14］) and auction results (［15］)
- **Records of regulations**: the CITES species database (［16］), 種の保存法 and its enforcement order (［17］［18］), pages and documents of the Ministry of the Environment and the Japan Wildlife Research Center (［19］〜［24］), and pages of the Ministry of the Environment and the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (［25］〜［27］)

## 2. Identity {#identity}

### The 1840 description

In 1840, S. Müller and H. Schlegel of the Netherlands described this fish as *Osteoglossum formosum*, based on fish from Borneo (［2］). It has since been moved to the genus *Scleropages*, and *Scleropages formosus* (Müller & Schlegel 1840) is the valid scientific name (［2］).

| Item | Details | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Name at description | *Osteoglossum formosum* Müller & Schlegel, 1840 (page 7, plate 1) | ［2］ |
| Current name | *Scleropages formosus* (Müller & Schlegel 1840). The valid scientific name | ［2］ |
| Type locality | A small lake on Borneo connected to a river by a narrow channel (Lake Mahoeroenge) | ［2］ |
| The specimen the name is based on | At a museum in the Netherlands (RMNH.PISC 3386a). Chosen in 2013 | ［2］ |
| Position in classification | Order Osteoglossiformes (the bonytongue order), family Osteoglossidae | ［2］［4］ |
| Other species of the same genus | *S. jardinii* and *S. leichardti* of Australia and New Guinea, and *S. inscriptus* of Myanmar (described in 2012) | ［2］ |
{caption="Records of the scientific name"}

- **Year of publication**: the catalog of fish scientific names says the year was corrected to 1840 in July 2013 (［2］). The 2003 paper (［1］) and the CITES species database (［16］) give 1844.
- **Origin of the name**: the genus name *Scleropages* comes from the Greek for "hard" (［4］［5］［6］). The species name *formosus* is Latin for "beautiful" (［5］［6］), and is said to come from the description of the body color as a beautiful green (［5］). However, the 2003 paper writes that it refers to its presence in Taiwan (Formosa) (［1］). Seriously Fish says it is sometimes written that it occurs in Taiwan, but it does not (［6］). Most sources give the Latin "beautiful" as the origin (inference).

### The 2003 paper that split it into 4 species

In 2003, researchers from France and Indonesia (Pouyaud, Sudarto and Teugels) published a paper arguing that the color forms of the Asian arowana are separate species (［1］). Their reason was that when part of a mitochondrial gene (cytochrome b) was compared, each color form made up its own group, and measurements of body shape agreed with this (［1］).

| Scientific name in the paper | Form in the trade | Locality given in the paper | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| *S. formosus* (in the narrow sense) | Green | Widely distributed in Southeast Asia | ［1］ |
| *S. macrocephalus* (new species) | Silver (Grey Tail, Yellow Tail) | Central Borneo (the Barito River) and western Borneo (the Melawi and Pinoh rivers, tributaries of the Kapuas River) | ［1］［2］ |
| *S. aureus* (new species) | Red Tail Golden | The Siak River (Pekanbaru) and the Batang Hari River (Jambi) on Sumatra | ［1］［2］ |
| *S. legendrei* (new species) | Super Red | Small lakes around Lake Sentarum on the upper Kapuas River, western Borneo | ［1］［2］ |
| (not split; kept as *S. formosus*) | Cross Back Golden (Blue Malay) | Pahang state and Lake Bukit Merah on the Malay Peninsula | ［1］［6］ |
{caption="How the 2003 paper divided them"}

- The paper said the 4 species can be told apart by proportions such as the length of the upper jaw bone, the height and width of the head, and the position of the fins (［1］). It examined 36 specimens, and writes that it could not obtain specimens of the Cross Back Golden (［1］).
- The paper also writes that it found hybrids (first generation), mainly between Super Red and Silver, at farms in Indonesia (［1］). Seriously Fish writes that the commonly seen hybrid "Banjar Red" is a cross between Super Red and Yellow Tail Silver (［6］).

### How it is treated now — mostly as 1 species

| Document | Treatment of the 3 species | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Catalog of fish scientific names (updated October 20, 2026) | All 3 are synonyms of *S. formosus*, following the treatment by Roberts in 2012 and by Kottelat in 2013 (with a question mark). It notes that there is also a work (2005) that treated the 3 as valid | ［2］ |
| CITES species database | Lists all 3 as synonyms of *S. formosus* | ［16］ |
| Seriously Fish | The current majority view is to treat all of them as *S. formosus* until a detailed revision appears. Kottelat (2013) writes that the red form may be a separate species | ［6］ |
| FishBase | Treated as the single species *S. formosus* | ［4］ |
{caption="How the 3 species are treated"}

The main text of Kottelat (2013) has not been opened, so on what grounds the question mark was added is **unverified**. None of the sources has decided that they are "not separate species"; the treatment is that "there is not yet enough evidence to split them" (inference).

The enforcement order of Japan's 種の保存法 (Act on Conservation of Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora) lists *Scleropages formosus* (アジアアロワナ, Asian arowana) and *Scleropages inscriptus*, and does not list the names of the 3 species (［18］). The CITES species database treats the 3 as synonyms of *S. formosus*, and also groups the Appendix I records under *S. formosus* (［16］). For this reason, forms such as 紅龍 are thought to be treated as *S. formosus* for regulatory purposes (inference).

### Appearance and sexes

- There are 21–26 lateral-line scales (［1］［6］), fewer than in the 2 Australian species (34–36［1］, 32–36［6］).
- The Malaysian research institute writes that males have slimmer, shallower bodies, a larger mouth and a deeper lower jaw. It says the large mouth is for holding and raising the eggs in the mouth (［8］). It says the difference between the sexes cannot be seen until they mature at around 3–4 years (［8］).

## 3. Habitat {#habitat}

### Distribution

| Document | What it says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Catalog of fish scientific names | Southeast Asia. Introduced to Hong Kong and the Philippines | ［2］ |
| FishBase | From southern Myanmar to the Malay Peninsula and Indonesia, and from eastern Thailand to the Cardamom Mountains | ［4］ |
| Seriously Fish | The Mekong River system (Vietnam, Cambodia), eastern Thailand, the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra and Borneo. It has disappeared from many places. Introduced to Singapore | ［6］ |
| CITES species database | Cambodia, Christmas Island, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, Vietnam | ［16］ |
{caption="Descriptions of the distribution"}

FishBase's "southern Myanmar" may overlap with the area of *S. inscriptus*, which was described as a separate species in 2012 (the Tanintharyi River system in Myanmar, ［2］) (inference).

### Where it lives and the water

- Seriously Fish says it lives in natural lakes, swamps, flooded forests and slow-flowing deep stretches of rivers, in places overhung by vegetation. It says some forms live in water stained brown by peat and the like (［6］). FishBase also lists dark, tannin-stained streams and forest rivers near peatlands (［4］).
- The authors of the 2003 paper write the following as their field observations in Indonesia (［1］).
  - Super Red lives only in dark, tannin-stained water **below pH 5.5** in small forest lakes around Lake Sentarum.
  - Red Tail Golden also lives in similar water, in lakes around the Siak and Batang Hari rivers on Sumatra.
  - Green and Silver live in small streams of whitish or clear water **above pH 6**, and were not collected in the same places as the 2 above.

### Water temperature — no measurements found

No record that measured the water temperature of the habitat was found in the sources opened this time. The guidelines for keeping are 22–28 °C (［6］) and 24–30 °C (［4］), 2 °C apart.

### Breeding in the wild

A 1976 paper that studied the Tasek Bera system in Pahang, Malaysia, says in its abstract that it spawns in the rainy season beginning in August, and that the young grow in the male's mouth until they can swim (［3］). It says males mature at 30–45 cm in the year after they pass 2 years of age, that all fish spawn at 45–60 cm in the year after they pass 3 years of age, and that a ripe ovary contained 20–30 eggs 1.9 cm in diameter (［3］). The main text has not been opened, so this is **unverified**. The Malaysian research institute writes that around Lake Bukit Merah the breeding season is August–October (［8］).

### Why it has declined

- Seriously Fish writes that since the 1970s it has been traded at high prices as an ornamental fish, and has disappeared from a large part of its range (［6］).
- The authors of the 2003 paper write that in a field survey in 2000, Super Red had probably disappeared from the south side of Lake Sentarum, and was known only from small forest lakes of less than 1 km² on the protected east side (［1］). From what fishermen and officials told them, they also write that the decline was due less to overcollection than to changes in the water (higher pH and cloudier water) caused by the logging of the forests (［1］; from interviews).
- On the IUCN (International Union for Conservation of Nature) Red List, it is "Endangered (EN)" as shown on FishBase, assessed on June 3, 2019 (［4］). The IUCN page itself has not been opened, so this is **unverified**.
- No figure counting how many remain in the wild was found in the sources opened this time.

### What the records show when lined up

- **Very few, very large eggs**: the number of eggs or young per spawn was 10–20 (［7］), 20–35 (［8］) and 16 (［9］). The 2003 paper, citing farmers, gives 50–150 for Green and fewer than 50 for Silver (［1］). Egg diameters are recorded as about 1 cm (［1］), 8–10 mm (［8］) and about 15 mm (［7］). The owner of a specialist shop in Japan writes, "Arowana are fish with extremely few eggs." (translated) (［11］).
- **About 2 months in the male's mouth**: the eggs hatch about 1 week after fertilization (［4］［7］［8］), and the young stay in the male's mouth until the yolk sac is gone. It is about 8 weeks until they leave the mouth (［8］［9］). During that time the male does not eat (［8］［9］).
- **Already large at birth**: the young leaving the mouth are 6–7 cm (［1］), about 7 cm and about 10 g (［9］), and about 6 cm (［4］).
- **Few spawnings a year**: the specialist shop's owner writes that they spawn 1–2 times a year (［11］). The Malaysian research institute says they spawn all year round, most often from July to December (［8］).

- How the eggs were counted differs: the number taken out, the number laid, or the number in the ovary (inference).
- In the Malaysian research institute's record, the reprinted text says "4550mm", which appears to be a miswriting of 45–50 mm. Elsewhere in the same record it says "about 8.5 cm", and which is correct is not known (［8］).

### Time in the mouth — how the shop and the institute differ

The owner of the specialist shop in Japan writes that the male is usually left to hold the eggs for 3–4 weeks after spawning (［11］). The Malaysian research institute also describes a method of taking the young out of the parent's mouth on day 30 and raising them in a tank (［8］). Left alone, they leave the mouth after about 8 weeks (［8］［9］), but farms may often take them out partway (inference).

### Breeding in tanks in Japan

- **Artificial hatching**: a specialist shop in Japan writes that in 2001 it had a pair spawn twice in a row in a tank, took the eggs out of the male's mouth 1–3 days after spawning, hatched them artificially, and raised just under 60 in total (［11］). The same shop's 2025 blog post gives the first success as 2000 and 50 or more from the 2 spawnings, so the year and the number differ slightly (［12］).
- **Many failures**: the same owner writes that over the following 5 years, they tried artificial hatching of more than 300 eggs in about 10 attempts and almost all failed, and that failures within 1 week of spawning made up more than 80% (［11］). A tropical fish shop in Osaka Prefecture also writes that as of 2012, the male would get as far as holding the eggs but ate them every time (［13］).
- **From Japan-born parents**: the same specialist shop writes that in August 2025 it hatched 9 artificially from Japan-born parents bred in the shop, and 4 of them were albino (［12］).
- The tropical fish shop in Osaka Prefecture writes that as of 2012, there were only a handful of successes in tanks in Japan (［13］). No record counting how many cases there are was found.

## 5. Keeping and breeding {#care}

### The short answer

Lining up the records, the keys to keeping them are as follows (inference).

1. **A very large tank** (at least 2 m long, ［6］)
2. **A secure lid** (they jump a lot, ［6］［10］)
3. **Stable water** (do not put them in a newly set-up tank; change 30–50% of the water weekly, ［6］)
4. **Plenty of oxygen** (［6］)

### Keeping conditions by source

Since there are no measurements from the habitat (chapter 3), all of these are guidelines meaning "the people who kept them managed with this" (inference). According to the 2003 paper, the pH of the habitat differs between forms (［1］), so the suitable water may differ by form (inference).

### Food

- In the wild it is a predator that eats small fish, crustaceans and the like (［6］). Young fish are said to eat insects at the surface, and adults fish, frogs and large insects (［1］［4］).
- In captivity, the data sheet recommends giving young fish bloodworms, small earthworms and chopped shrimp, and adults fish fillets, shrimp, shellfish and the like, and not making dry foods the staple (［6］).
- Seriously Fish writes not to give the meat of mammals or birds, such as beef heart or chicken, because the fish cannot make good use of the fat and it is bad for them, and that feeder goldfish and the like may bring in disease (［6］).

### Size and growth

- The maximum is given as 90.0 cm total length (［4］) and 900 mm standard length (［6］). Auckland Zoo says it reaches up to 90 cm and 6.8 kg (［9］).
- The 2003 paper writes that Green usually does not exceed 60 cm total length, and that Silver rarely exceeds 40 cm in ponds (［1］).
- Maturity is given as 3 years (［1］) and from the 4th year (45–60 cm) (［8］).
- The UK magazine article says it takes up to 1 year for the young to reach a size that can be sold (［7］).

### Breeding

Thinking from the records, the clues for breeding are as follows (inference).

- The parents take about 4 years to mature, so it takes a long time (［1］［8］).
- Before spawning there is courtship lasting several weeks to several months, in which the 2 fish swim near the surface at night or swim side by side with their bodies close together (［4］［8］).
- The first week after the male takes the eggs into his mouth is the hardest (［11］［13］).
- There is a method of taking the eggs out partway (［8］［11］) and a method of leaving them to the male to the end (［9］).
- To pass on young bred in Japan to others, the parents must be registered, and the young must also be registered (chapter 6, ［21］).

## 6. Before you buy {#before-buying}

### Is it really a rare fish?

Lining up the records, **although there are expensive individuals of particular forms, the Asian arowana itself is a fish that is bred in captivity and traded in large numbers** (inference).

- Indonesia has notified CITES of export quotas for live captive-bred specimens for each year from 2024 to 2026: 267,188 of the red form, 101,054 of the golden form, 12,098 Green and 25,610 Banjar (［16］).
- In Japan, there were 262,455 registrations of live specimens under 種の保存法 from 1993 to May 2016, of which 254,611 were Asian arowana (［22］). By the author's calculation, that is about 97%.
- Registrations in 2017–2023 rose from 6,347 fish in 2017 and 5,498 in 2018 to 8,774 in 2020. A Ministry of the Environment document says this may be the effect of "stay-at-home demand" (translated) during the COVID-19 pandemic (［23］).
- A Ministry of the Environment document says that for species that have CITES-registered breeding facilities, such as the Asian arowana, imports and registrations have continued steadily since the regulations (［24］).

On the other hand, wild fish have disappeared from many places in the range (［6］), and the habitat of Super Red is limited to small lakes (［1］). What is rare is thought to be the wild fish, not the fish being sold (inference).

### Telling the sexes apart

- Males have slimmer bodies, a larger mouth and a deeper lower jaw (［8］).
- Below the male's mouth is a pouch-like part that swells when he is raising eggs (［8］). A male holding eggs has swollen gill covers, does not eat, and becomes quiet (［8］).
- Young fish cannot be told apart (［8］).

### Legality and regulation {#legal}

The Asian arowana is listed in CITES Appendix I, and in Japan it is a 国際希少野生動植物種 (Internationally Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora) under 種の保存法 (Act on Conservation of Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora). If you follow the rules, you can buy and keep a fish that has a registration card. However, **this page cannot confirm whether each individual fish was really bred at a registered breeding facility and correctly exported, imported and registered, and it cannot state whether each fish in the trade is legal.** What is known and what is not known are written separately below.

#### What is known

- **Changes in the CITES Appendix listing**: according to the CITES species database (Species+), it was listed in Appendix I on July 1, 1975. On January 18, 1990, only the Indonesian population (ranching) was moved to Appendix II, and the rest stayed in Appendix I. On February 16, 1995, all populations were placed in Appendix I. The record of September 25, 2012 carries the note "includes *S. inscriptus*", and the current listing is dated January 2, 2017 (［16］). The supplementary provisions of the amendment to Japan's enforcement order that took effect on February 16, 1995 also contain transitional measures for Asian arowana of the Indonesian population (［18］).
- **What Appendix I means**: the Japan Wildlife Research Center explains that 国際希少野生動植物種 are species whose international trade for commercial purposes is prohibited under CITES and other agreements (［21］). FishBase also shows Appendix I as "international trade prohibited" (［4］).
- **Export of captive-bred fish**: Indonesia has notified CITES of export quotas for captive-bred specimens (［16］). A Ministry of the Environment document gives "specimens bred at CITES-registered breeding facilities, etc." (translated) as an example of a requirement for registration (［22］). Seriously Fish writes that there are several CITES-registered breeders in Asia, and that legally exported fish come with a microchip and official papers (［6］).
- **Designation under 種の保存法**: Table 2 of Appended Table 2 of the enforcement order lists *Scleropages formosus* (アジアアロワナ, Asian arowana) and *Scleropages inscriptus*. For both, the "date of application" (translated) is November 4, 1980 (Showa 55) (［18］).
- **Ban on trade and advertising**: 種の保存法 prohibits the transfer, etc. (selling, buying, giving, receiving, lending, borrowing and so on) of specimens of 国際希少野生動植物種, and their display or advertising for the purpose of sale or distribution. Registered specimens are an exception (Articles 12 and 17, ［17］［20］). The Japan Wildlife Research Center says that buying, selling and displaying on auction sites must also wait until the registration card has been issued (［21］).
- **Which specimens can be registered**: the enforcement order sets out what can be registered, including specimens bred in Japan, specimens acquired or imported in Japan before the date of application, and specimens imported with permission under 関税法 (Customs Act) and bred for commercial purposes (Article 8, ［18］). The Japan Wildlife Research Center lists the Asian arowana as a main species in the "bred in Japan" and "imported" categories (［21］).
- **Registration procedure**: registration is carried out by the Japan Wildlife Research Center, the registration body for specimens (［19］［21］). It says the fee is 5,000 yen per specimen and processing takes about 1–3 months (checked November 18, 2026, ［21］). It says registering an imported specimen requires, besides a copy of the import papers, the export permit of the country of origin and a list of microchip numbers (［21］). From January 2026, a copy of an identity document is also required (［21］).
- **Validity of registration**: registration of a live specimen is valid for 5 years; if it is not renewed it lapses, and transferring the fish while it has lapsed is a violation. Renewal can be applied for from 6 months before the expiry date, and the fee is 4,600 yen per specimen (［17］［21］). Even after it lapses, the owner can continue to keep the fish themselves (［21］).
- **Rules after registration**: when transferring a fish, it must go together with its registration card; when displaying it for sale, the registration card must be kept with it; and advertisements must state that it is registered, among other matters (Article 21, ［17］). A Ministry of the Environment document says that advertisements show the registration number (［22］). The buyer must report within 30 days, and when the fish dies or the like, the registration card must be returned within 30 days (Articles 21 and 22, ［17］［21］).
- **What a registration card means**: the Ministry of the Environment says the registration card proves that the specimen is registered, and does not guarantee the species name (［19］).
- **Young bred in Japan**: to register the young, the parents must as a rule be registered. If you keep them yourself for life, the young do not need to be registered, but registration is recommended if you might transfer them (［21］).
- **Microchips**: under Japanese law, individual identification (such as a microchip) at registration is required only for mammals, birds, reptiles and the genus *Andrias* (giant salamanders) (［21］). In a table in a Ministry of the Environment document, individual identification for the Asian arowana is also "not required" (translated) (［23］). However, there is a record of microchips being implanted at farms that export the fish (［7］).
- **Penalties**: transfer, etc. without registration carries imprisonment of up to 5 years or a fine of up to 5 million yen (sometimes both), and a fine of up to 100 million yen for a corporation (Articles 57-2 and 65, ［17］). Display or advertising for sale without registration carries imprisonment of up to 1 year or a fine of up to 1 million yen, and a fine of up to 20 million yen for a corporation (Articles 58 and 65, ［17］). Violations concerning registration cards (such as transferring without the registration card or not reporting) carry a fine of up to 300,000 yen (Article 63, ［17］).
- **IUCN Red List**: "Endangered (EN)" as shown on FishBase, assessed on June 3, 2019 (［4］).

#### What is not known

- Which farm in which country the fish on sale were bred at, and with which papers they were imported, is not written in the shop or auction listings and is not known (［14］［15］). Even with a registration card, the species name is not guaranteed (［19］).
- CITES's register of facilities that breed Appendix I animals for commercial purposes could not be opened because the CITES site showed a verification screen (which was not solved), so the number and names of the registered farms are **unverified**.
- If the 3 species of the 2003 paper are recognized as valid species again in the future, how CITES and 種の保存法 would treat them is not known.
- The text of the enforcement regulations (Article 11-3) on what must be shown in advertisements has not been opened, and this was written only from the Ministry of the Environment document (［22］) (**unverified**).
- Seriously Fish writes that trade is not legal in the US and elsewhere (［6］). The US law itself has not been checked, so this is **unverified**.
- The 2008 news report says keeping is permitted only in Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore (［10］), but in Japan too fish with registration cards can be bought and sold (［17］［19］), so this description is not accurate (inference).
- Whether it is covered by prefectural ordinances has not been checked yet (**unverified**).
- So this page cannot tell whether each fish in the trade was legally bred, exported, imported and registered.

#### Japanese regulations: date checked

Checked on 2026-11-18.

- **種の保存法 (Act on Conservation of Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora)**: on e-Gov Laws and Regulations Search, it was checked that Table 2 of Appended Table 2 of the enforcement order (the version in force from March 5, 2026) lists *Scleropages formosus* (アジアアロワナ, Asian arowana) and *Scleropages inscriptus*, and Articles 12, 17 and 20–22 and the penalties (Articles 57-2, 58, 63 and 65) of the Act (the version in force from July 1, 2026) were checked (［17］［18］). **It is a 国際希少野生動植物種, and a registration card is required to buy or sell it.**

- **動物愛護管理法 (Act on Welfare and Management of Animals; specified animals)**: according to the Ministry of the Environment's page, specified animals are about 650 species of mammals, birds and reptiles, such as tigers, bears, crocodiles and mamushi vipers, and fish are not covered (［26］).
- **Import permission under 水産資源保護法 (Fishery Resources Protection Act)**: on the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries page (updated June 9, 2026), the arowana group was not among the fish that require import permission (［27］).
- **Washington Convention (CITES)**: Appendix I (［16］). The Japan Wildlife Research Center advises that import procedures are handled by the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry (［21］).
- **Others** (such as prefectural ordinances) have not been checked yet (**unverified**).

#### This page's position

> **This page does not state whether each fish in the trade was legally bred, exported, imported and registered.** As above, it sets out what is known and what is not known separately. When buying or selling an Asian arowana in Japan, please check on the pages of the Japan Wildlife Research Center and the Ministry of the Environment that the fish and its registration card must go together, that you must report within 30 days of buying, and that registration of a live specimen is valid for 5 years.

## 7. What is still unknown {#unknowns}

Below are the gaps that the sources collected this time could not fill, and the points marked "(unverified)" or "(needs checking)" in the text.

1. **Classification**: on what grounds Kottelat (2013) made the 3 species of 2003 (［1］) questionable synonyms is **unverified**, because the main text could not be opened. Whether the red form is a separate species has not yet been settled (［2］［6］).
2. **Origin of the name**: the sources split between the Latin "beautiful" (［5］［6］) and Taiwan (Formosa) (［1］).
3. **Water temperature of the habitat**: no measured record has been found. The keeping guidelines also differ: 22–28 °C (［6］) and 24–30 °C (［4］).
4. **Numbers in the wild**: no figure counting how many remain has been found. The reason given for the decline of Super Red (changes in the water from logging) comes from interviews with fishermen and others (［1］). The IUCN page is **unverified**.
5. **Breeding in the wild**: only the abstract of the 1976 paper (［3］) was read; the main text is **unverified**.
6. **Number and size of eggs**: they differ greatly between sources, from 10–20 (［7］) to 50–150 (［1］), and from 8–10 mm (［8］) to about 15 mm (［7］) in diameter. Whether this is due to differences in how they were counted is not known either.
7. **The Malaysian research institute's record**: the original report (FRI Bulletin, 2000) could not be opened and it was read in a reprint (［8］), so it is **unverified**. The figures for the size of the young conflict within the record.
8. **Number of tank breedings in Japan**: there is no record counting how many cases there are. Even within the specialist shop's records, the year of the first success (2000 or 2001) and the number (50 or more, or just under 60) conflict (［11］［12］). The magazine article written by the shop's owner (「プラタパッド」 No. 29, cited in［11］) is **unverified**.
9. **The November 2012 spawning at the Osaka Prefecture shop**: what happened afterward is not reported. The number of eggs is the shop's estimate (［13］; **needs checking**).
10. **CITES-registered breeding facilities**: the register could not be opened, so the number and names of the farms are **unverified**.
11. **Text of the enforcement regulations**: what must be shown in advertisements (Article 11-3) is **unverified**.
12. **Dates of the Ministry of the Environment documents**: the PDFs of the documents on registration numbers (［22］〜［24］) do not say which meeting they were for or when they were issued. ［23］ says its figures run from 2017 to May 2024.

14. **Japanese regulations**: prefectural ordinances are **unverified**.
