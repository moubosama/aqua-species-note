The Arc red pencilfish is a small pencilfish in which the space between the black lengthwise stripes is colored red (［16］［19］). Japanese articles introduce it as expensive, because it is very hard to breed and only wild fish (fish collected in the wild) are on the market: "One reason it is rare is that breeding it is very difficult." (translated) (［18］).

This page first checks **which fish the name "Arc red pencil" refers to**, and then examines whether the claims that it is "hard to breed" and that "only wild fish are on the market" can be confirmed from the records.

This page was made by collecting and cross-checking the following kinds of information.

- **Papers, research databases and data sheets**: the 2001 original description (［1］; its main text could not be opened, and the bibliographic details were checked in［2］), a catalog of fish scientific names (［2］), FishBase (［3］), Seriously Fish (［4］), the IUCN Red List (［5］; its text has not been opened), and the abstract of the paper describing the related species *N. rubrocaudatus* (［6］)
- **Articles and forums from abroad**: a US aquarium magazine (［7］［8］), a reposted article from a UK magazine (［9］), pages of a German ornamental fish importer (［10］［11］), forums (［12］［13］), news in a US aquarium magazine (［14］), and a Spanish-language fish encyclopedia site (［15］)
- **Records from Japan**: a keeper's keeping example (［16］), a keeper's blog (［17］), articles (［18］〜［22］), shop blogs and pages (［23］〜［30］), and auction results (［31］)
- **Records of regulations**: the CITES species database (［32］), pages of the Ministry of the Environment and Japanese law (［33］〜［36］), and a Peruvian guide to prohibited species (［37］)

## 2. Identity {#identity}

### Conclusion — a trade name for the described species *Nannostomus mortenthaleri*

To give the conclusion first: **the fish called "Arc red pencil" in Japan is, in most sources, the Peruvian pencilfish *Nannostomus mortenthaleri*, described in 2001.** (［1］［2］ and the table below) It is not an undescribed species (sp.), nor the name of a pattern type within a species.

| Document | Scientific name given for "Arc red" | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Column by a keeper in Japan (2022; with a keeping example written with a shop's cooperation) | *Nannostomus mortenthaleri* (English name Coral red pencilfish) | ［16］ |
| Japanese tropical fish explanation site (2024) | *Nannostomus mortenthaleri* | ［19］ |
| Fish guide page of a Japanese shop (updated 2019) | *Nannostomus mortenthaleri* Paepke & Arendt, 2001 | ［25］ |
| Product information field of a Japanese online shop | Nannostomus mortenthaleri | ［29］ |
| Japanese articles and summary sites (2019–2022) | *Nannostomus mortenthaleri* | ［21］［22］ |
| Product page of a Japanese shop (checked November 18, 2026) | *Nannostomus rubrocaudatus* (the shop itself writes "needs checking") | ［30］ |
| Data sheet from abroad | The English trade name 'red arc' is another name for *N. mortenthaleri* | ［4］ |
{caption="How the name Arc red matches scientific names"}

Only 1 shop gives the scientific name of a different, related species, *N. rubrocaudatus* (［30］). The Japanese keeper's column gives the common Japanese name of *N. rubrocaudatus* as「ブラッドレッドペンシルフィッシュ」(Blood red pencilfish), and lists it as a fish different from the Arc red (［16］). So **although the match between the name and the scientific name is mostly settled, it is not necessarily the same at every shop**.

### How the names became mixed up

Red pencilfish have been confused by name since the early 2000s (［9］［11］).

- According to the UK magazine article, this fish was first imported into Europe under the names *Nannostomus* sp. "Peru" and "Peru Red". The article writes that exporters later also shipped another red pencilfish under the names "Peru Red" and *N. marginatus* "Red", and that importers said they could not tell which species would arrive when they ordered (［9］).
- The German importer writes that red pencilfish that came from Peru in 2002 under the names *N. marginatus* "Purple" and "Red" were described in 2009 as *N. rubrocaudatus* (［11］). The abstract of that describing paper says that the new species is close to *N. mortenthaleri* and *N. marginatus*, and that the color of live males differs greatly from these 2 species (［6］).
- In 2022, a Peruvian exporter introduced a new pencilfish, red over almost its whole body, as *Nannostomus* sp. 'Super Red Cenepa'. Researchers have suggested it may be an unusual individual of *N. rubrocaudatus* (［14］). In Japan too, in the same year, a new red pencilfish from Peru (「クリムゾンレッドペンシル」(Crimson red pencil)) was reported to have arrived (［16］).

In other words, **"red Peruvian pencilfish" include at least 2 species and a type of unclear identity, so deciding what a fish is from its name is risky** (inference, from［6］［9］［14］).

### Where the name "Arc red" comes from

No source was found that explains where the Japanese name「アークレッド」(Arc red) comes from. There is an English trade name 'red arc pencilfish' (［4］［7］), and the Japanese keeper's column gives the English spelling as "Ark red pencilfish" (［16］). It may be the English name with the words swapped round (inference), but this has not been confirmed.

### How to tell it from similar species

The species most easily confused with it is *N. rubrocaudatus* (common Japanese name「ブラッドレッドペンシル」(Blood red pencil)［16］).

| What to look at | *N. mortenthaleri* (Arc red) | *N. rubrocaudatus* | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Red on the male's body | Red between the upper 2 black stripes. Outside the breeding season, the red is only in this band, including on the rear half of the body | The red on the rear half of the body is purplish and spreads evenly | ［4］ |
| Male's dorsal fin | A white spot in front of the base; red tip | Red at the front; clear tip | ［4］ |
| Pelvic and anal fins | Red almost all over | Red at the base; clear tips | ［4］ |
| Belly | Always white | Not white | ［11］ |
{caption="Telling the Arc red from N. rubrocaudatus"}

- The dwarf pencilfish (*N. marginatus*) is easy to tell apart, because the male has no red on the body (［4］).
- A Japanese keeper writes that the Arc red is a size larger than the Blood red and that its teeth are noticeable. The keeper also says that fights between males are fiercer in the Arc red (［16］).
- At night the colors become very pale, but the black stripes remain (［4］［16］). This is not a disease (［16］).

## 3. Habitat {#habitat}

### A very small range

| Document | What it says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Catalog of fish scientific names | The Nanay River basin, Loreto, Peru | ［2］ |
| Seriously Fish | Apparently endemic to the Nanay River basin; also in the Tigre River system a little to the west | ［4］ |
| US magazine article | Tributaries of the middle and upper Nanay River | ［8］ |
| UK magazine article | Forest tributaries of the middle and upper Nanay River | ［9］ |
| Japanese articles | The Tigre River (basin), Peru | ［19］［20］ |
{caption="Descriptions of the distribution"}

Japanese articles give the Tigre River (［19］［20］), but the type locality is on the Nanay River (［2］). Some fish at Japanese shops are also labeled "Rio Nanay" (［27］).

### Where it lives

- Seriously Fish writes that it lives in slow-flowing tributaries, small streams and swamps, and is most common among dense aquatic plants and in places with sunken wood and fallen leaves. It describes the water as shallow and almost still, with almost no hardness and low electrical conductivity, at pH 4.0–6.0 (［4］).
- The same page gives the water when 2 people from Norway and the UK collected it in a forest stream in the Tigre River basin in 2010 as pH 4.54–5.78, electrical conductivity 11 µS/cm and water temperature 25.3–26.8 °C (［4］). The collectors' original record has not been opened and is **unverified**. Fish found with it included *Nannostomus eques*, *Pyrrhulina spilota*, *Crenuchus spilurus* and Apistogramma species (［4］).
- No record measuring the water at the type locality in the Nanay River basin was found in the sources opened this time.

### Concern about wild numbers

FishBase shows it as **"Critically Endangered (CR)"** on the Red List of the IUCN (International Union for Conservation of Nature), under criteria B1ab(v), assessed on April 24, 2014 (［3］). These criteria mean that the range is small and the number of mature fish appears to be continuing to decline (inference; the reading of the criteria is the author's).

The text of the IUCN assessment has not been opened (**unverified**). A Spanish-language fish encyclopedia site describes the content of the IUCN assessment as a range of about 100 km², concentrated in 1 place, with collecting for the aquarium trade as a threat (［15］; **unverified**). The same site also writes that exports varied greatly in 2000–2012, from a few dozen fish a year to more than 30,000, with 31,183 in 2012 (［15］; **unverified**). It does not say which statistics this comes from.

This site says the species is not in any protected area (［15］). However, Seriously Fish has a photo said to have been taken in the Allpahuayo-Mishana National Reserve just south of Iquitos (［4］), so the two conflict.

## 4. Breeding records compared {#breeding}

The table above lists records of breeding in captivity, one record per row. **All 4 are records from abroad; no record of it being bred in Japan was found.** For the 1st record (［8］), the original text was opened on November 18, 2026 to check the numbers and descriptions carried into the table. The Czech record (［9］) is an account given in an excerpt of an article and the forum record (［13］) is hearsay, so both "need checking"; the first report of 2001 (［9］) is "unverified" because the original report could not be opened. The inch conversions are by the author.

### Is it really "very hard to breed"?

Sources put it differently.

| Document | What it says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Japanese article (published 2022, updated 2023) | Breeding is very difficult, so only wild fish are on the market | ［18］ |
| Japanese article (2019) | It is not impossible to get them to spawn in an aquarium, but the fry are very small and hard to raise. Best to assume that breeding in an ordinary aquarium is not possible | ［21］ |
| Japanese summary site (updated 2020) | Hard to breed, and almost all fish in Japan are wild. In a densely planted tank where tiny organisms appear naturally, they may sometimes breed on their own | ［22］ |
| Species profile in a US magazine | Breeding has only just begun, but getting them to spawn does not seem very difficult | ［7］ |
| Breeding article in a US magazine (2009) | They spawn less often than other species. Breeding them is a challenge for the ambitious | ［8］ |
| Seriously Fish | Breeding in aquariums is rare but has succeeded. In a mature planted tank, a few fry may appear without any intervention | ［4］ |

{caption="Descriptions of how hard it is to breed"}

Lined up, the most common view seems to be that **"getting them to spawn" is not difficult, but "raising the fry" and "breeding them in numbers" are** (inference). "Very difficult" (［18］) may be an overstatement, but it is true that no record of breeding in Japan has been found.

### What the records show when lined up

- **The method is almost the same as for other pencilfish**: keeping the eggs away from the parents (gaps on the bottom, a wool spawning mop, removing the parents after spawning) is common to the article (［8］), the forums (［12］［13］) and the data sheet (［4］). Using soft water is also common to them (［8］［12］［13］).
- **The first food differs between sources**: some sources start with very small food such as infusoria (［4］［12］［13］), while the article says that the fry of this species are larger than those of the dwarf pencilfish and soon eat brine shrimp nauplii and similar food (［8］).
- **The water numbers conflict**: the breeding article gives pH 6.8 and 24 °C as ideal (［8］). The forum post gives very soft water with a hardness under 1 degree (［13］; **needs checking**).
- **How the fry grow**: the breeding article says they mature at 6–7 months (［8］), but a forum post says that growth is very slow for the first 3 months and that it takes 9–10 months to reach breeding size (［12］; it does not say whether the poster bred them, so it **needs checking**). Seriously Fish says that the adult pattern appears from about 8 weeks (［4］).
- **Tank-born fish are on the market**: the US magazine's species profile writes that tank-bred fish have become available and that prices are falling (［7］). The German importer's stock list has a separate number for "Offspring" (young born in an aquarium), apart from wild fish (［10］).

## 5. Keeping and breeding {#care}

### The short answer

Lining up the records, the keys to keeping them are as follows (inference).

1. **Space and hiding places, to cope with fights between males** (［4］［9］［16］)
2. **Acclimatize them slowly when introducing them** (［18］［20］［21］)
3. **Around 25–26 °C, avoiding high temperatures** (［16］［21］)
4. **Small food that fits in their mouths** (［16］［20］)

### Keeping conditions by source

| Source | Temperature | pH | Hardness etc. | Other |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seriously Fish［4］ | 24–28 °C | 4.0–7.0 | 1–5 dGH | 90 × 30 cm or more (if keeping males and females together). Preferably 10 or more fish |
| German importer［10］ | 22–28 °C | Soft, slightly acidic water suits it, but it adapts to various waters | No record | A group of 10–20, in a larger tank |

| Japanese tropical fish explanation site［19］ | 22–27 °C | Suitable 6.0–7.0 (survivable 5.5–7.5) | No record | 60–90 cm planted tank |
| Japanese article［21］ | 22–26 °C | 5.5–7 | No record | Weak against high temperatures |
| Japanese summary site［22］ | 24–28 °C | No record | No record | No record |
{caption="Keeping conditions by source"}

The upper temperature limit differs between sources, from 26 °C (［21］) to 28 °C (［4］［10］［22］). None of the numbers say what they are based on. The habitat measurement of 25.3–26.8 °C (［4］; the collectors' original record is **unverified**) falls within the range of most sources, but slightly exceeds the upper limit of the 22–26 °C range (［21］).

### Fights within the species

- Males are said to be very aggressive toward their own kind, and may even kill each other in cramped conditions (［4］［9］). The author of the UK magazine article obtained 10 of the first fish imported in 2002, but lost them one after another in a small tank, and the last male then lived alone for 2 years (［9］).
- The German importer writes that a clear hierarchy forms in a group: only the dominant males are red, and weaker males take on female-like colors (［10］). Japanese sources also say that the strongest male becomes the reddest (［19］［20］［21］).
- On the other hand, a Japanese keeper's blog records that 2 males hardly fought at all (［17］). It may depend on the number of males and the size of the tank (inference).
- It is said to show little interest in other kinds of fish (［20］), but may attack pencilfish of similar shape and color (［10］［19］).

### Food

- The mouth is very small, so give small or crushed food (［16］［20］［21］).
- A Japanese keeper says that all the fish soon got used to prepared food, given once or twice a day until the belly is slightly rounded (［16］). The UK magazine article writes that the wild fish that first appeared on the Czechoslovak market were thin and would eat only brine shrimp nauplii (［9］).

### Lifespan

Japanese articles give 2–3 years (［20］［21］), but they do not give the original records. The UK magazine article records that the last remaining male lived for a further 2 years after the author obtained it (［9］).

### Breeding

Based on the records in chapter 4, here is a method worth trying (inference).

1. Raise the parents in a group of about 10, and choose a pair that has spawned within the group (［8］［10］)
2. Before spawning, keep the male and female apart for 7–10 days and feed plenty of live food (［8］)
3. Fill a small tank with soft water, with gaps on the bottom for the eggs to fall into and a wool spawning mop or Java moss above. Keep the lower part of the tank dark (［8］［13］). One article gives pH 6.8 as ideal (［8］), and one post says acidic water (［12］)
4. Remove the parents after spawning. The parents eat the eggs (［8］［12］)
5. Start the fry on small food such as infusoria, then move on to brine shrimp nauplii (［4］［12］［13］). One article says that the fry of this species soon eat brine shrimp nauplii (［8］)

## 6. Before you buy {#before-buying}

### Is it really a rare fish?

While one article calls it "rare" (［18］), a keeper in Japan wrote in 2022 that the Arc red is one of the more widely traded pencilfish and should not be hard to obtain (［16］). The Japanese tropical fish explanation site also rates its availability as "average" (translated) (［19］).

### Wild or tank-born?

- All the Japanese sales records seen this time were labeled「ワイルド」(wild) or "Wild" (［24］［27］［28］［29］［30］). No record of fish bred in Japan being sold was found.
- Abroad, tank-bred fish are on the market (［7］［10］). The UK magazine article writes that tank-born fish are less aggressive toward their own kind than wild fish (［9］).

### Telling the sexes apart

- Adult males are slimmer than females, with much brighter colors. Males have a white mark in front of the base of the dorsal fin from a young age (［4］).
- The German importer writes that in pencilfish the shape of the anal fin is the most reliable way to tell the sexes apart, but that in this species the difference is slight: the front edge of the male's anal fin bulges slightly, while the female's is straight. Females lack the white streak at the base of the dorsal fin (［10］).
- Weaker males take on female-like colors, so color alone is said to make the sexes hard to tell apart (［10］).

### Cautions when introducing them

- Newly introduced fish are said to be sensitive to changes in water chemistry (［18］［19］［20］). One article recommends acclimatizing them slowly and watching them for about a week after introduction (［20］).
- Right after introduction their colors are pale, and they are said to turn red after being kept for a while (［19］).
- It cannot be ruled out that a different species arrives under the name "Arc red" (chapter 2; inference). It is a good idea to check whether the fish that arrive match the features in the table in chapter 2.

### Legality and regulation {#legal}

This species is not listed by CITES (the Washington Convention), and, as far as was checked this time, it is not among the species whose import or keeping is regulated by Japanese law either. However, this species is listed as "Critically Endangered (CR)" on the IUCN Red List (［3］), and **it is not known where, or under which permits, each fish in the trade was collected and exported, so this page cannot state whether the trade in wild fish was legal.** What is known and what is not known are written separately below.

#### What is known

- **CITES**: searching the species database of the CITES Secretariat and others (Species+) for *Nannostomus* (genus) and Lebiasinidae (family) gave no records (checked November 18, 2026) (［32］). FishBase also shows "Not Evaluated" (［3］).
- **IUCN Red List**: "Critically Endangered (CR)", criteria B1ab(v), assessed on April 24, 2014 (as shown on FishBase; ［3］). The IUCN Red List is not a law, and this assessment alone does not ban trade (inference).
- **Peru's prohibited species**: Peru's regulation on Amazonian fisheries (Reglamento de Ordenamiento Pesquero de la Amazonía Peruana, D.S. N° 015-2009-PRODUCE) lists species whose trade as ornamental fish is prohibited. A 2022 guide made by the Peruvian Amazon Research Institute (IIAP) and WCS (the Wildlife Conservation Society) covers the 35 species currently listed in this regulation, and neither this species nor the pencilfish family was among them (［37］). The 35 species are food fish landed in the Loreto and Ucayali regions, and the listing aims to stop their numbers falling because their young are caught as ornamental fish (［37］).
- **Export from Peru**: a Japanese shop's blog records fish that came from Peru (a shipment from Peru) (［23］). The UK magazine article writes that in the early days it was difficult to transport the fish to the export base in Lima (［9］).

#### What is not known

- What permits and documents are needed in Peru to collect and export ornamental fish that are not prohibited species, such as this one, is **unverified**, because the legal texts have not been opened.
- Whether the Peruvian government has restricted collecting of this species, or added it to a national list of threatened species, in response to the IUCN assessment has not been checked (**unverified**).
- The text of the IUCN assessment (threats, recommended measures) has not been opened. The export figures (［15］) are also **unverified**.
- From which exporter, and with what documents, the fish sold in Japan were imported is mostly not known, because it is not labeled (［27］〜［30］).
- Sources conflict on whether the places where this species lives include a protected area (［4］［15］). How collecting inside a protected area is treated is not known either.
- So it is not known whether the wild fish in the trade were collected and exported legally and in a sustainable way.

#### Japanese regulations: date checked

Checked on 2026-11-18.

- **Invasive Alien Species Act**: the Ministry of the Environment's "List of Invasive Alien Species, etc." was checked. The only fish on it are some members of the orders Lepisosteiformes (gars), Cypriniformes, Siluriformes, Esociformes, Cyprinodontiformes and Perciformes; Characiformes (the group that includes the pencilfish) were not listed as an Invasive Alien Species, an Uncategorized Alien Species, or an organism that requires a species identification certificate (［33］).
- **List of Alien Species That May Damage Ecosystems** (not a legal regulation, but a list to call for caution): neither pencilfish nor characins were in the file listing fish (［34］).
- **Act on Welfare and Management of Animals (Specified Animals)**: on the Ministry of the Environment's page, the Specified Animals are about 650 species of mammals, birds and reptiles, such as tigers, bears, crocodiles and mamushi (a pit viper) (［35］). The list of Specified Animals also covers only the classes Mammalia, Aves and Reptilia, and **fish are not covered** (［35］).
- **Act on Conservation of Endangered Species**: in the text of the enforcement order (e-Gov law search), neither the name *Nannostomus* nor the name of the pencilfish family was found (［36］).
- **Washington Convention (CITES)**: as above, this species is not listed (［32］).
- Whether this species is covered by **other rules** (such as import permits under the Fisheries Resources Protection Act, or prefectural ordinances) has not been checked yet (**unverified**).

#### This page's position

> **This page does not state whether the wild fish in the trade were legally collected and exported.** As above, it sets out what is known and what is not known separately. Not being regulated under Japanese law does not mean that collecting and export in the country of origin were legal. Please also be aware that this species is listed as "Critically Endangered (CR)" by the IUCN, and that collecting wild fish may be affecting a species with a small range.

## 7. What is still unknown {#unknowns}

Below are the gaps that the sources collected this time could not fill, and the points marked "(unverified)" or "(needs checking)" in the text.

1. **Matching the name to the species**: there is no definition of "Arc red". Most sources give *N. mortenthaleri*, but one shop gives *N. rubrocaudatus* (［30］). In the early 2000s, 2 species were sold mixed together under the name "Peru Red" (［9］).
2. **Where "Arc red" comes from**: its relationship to the English 'red arc' (［4］［7］) is only an inference.
3. **Main text of the original description**: the 2001 original description (［1］) has not been opened (**unverified**).
4. **Distribution**: sources differ between the Nanay River (［2］) and the Tigre River (［4］［19］［20］). Whether the Tigre River population has been confirmed to be the same species is not known either.
5. **Water of the habitat**: the original record of the measurements in the Tigre River system (［4］) is **unverified**. No measurements from the type locality on the Nanay River have been found.
6. **IUCN assessment**: its text has not been opened (**unverified**). The export figures (［15］) are also **unverified**. Sources conflict on whether it lives inside a protected area (［4］［15］).
7. **Breeding**: no record of breeding in Japan has been found. The first report abroad (2001, Bork) could not be opened (［9］, **unverified**). The Czech and UK records (［9］［13］) are hearsay and **need checking**.
8. **Growth rate of the fry**: maturity at 6–7 months (［8］) conflicts with 9–10 months to breeding size (［12］, **needs checking**).
9. **Lifespan**: what the 2–3 years (［20］［21］) is based on is not known.
10. **What is traded in Japan**: all the Japanese sales seen this time were labeled wild. Whether fish bred in Japan are on the market is not known.
11. **Rules in the country of origin**: the permits needed in Peru to collect and export ornamental fish, and whether the Peruvian government protects this species, are **unverified**.
12. **Japanese regulations**: the Fisheries Resources Protection Act and prefectural ordinances are **unverified**.
13. **Sources that could not be opened**: the original description (［1］), the IUCN Red List page (［5］; access was refused), the main text of the paper describing *N. rubrocaudatus* (［6］ is the abstract only; the PDF could not be obtained), the full text of the UK magazine article (［9］ is only a reposted excerpt), a checklist of the freshwater fishes of Loreto, Peru (2021; the PDF could not be obtained), and the product page of a major Japanese online shop (it could not be opened because it was under maintenance).

If you keep the Arc red pencilfish and record **the origin label (Nanay River or Tigre River), the identification features in chapter 2, the water temperature and chemistry, the numbers of males and females, how many years they lived, and whether you saw spawning or fry**, that will be very useful as a record that can be compared with others. In particular, not a single record of breeding in Japan has been found yet.
