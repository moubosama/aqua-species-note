The New black cherry tetra is a small characin about 3 cm long whose tail fin is tinged with red (［4］［9］). A Japanese article lists this fish as one of the rare tropical fish that are seldom on the market, and writes, "However, few are in circulation, and it is expensive and rare." (translated) (［4］).

This page first checks **which fish the name "New black cherry tetra" refers to**, and then examines whether the claims that "few are in circulation" and that it is "expensive" can be confirmed from the records.

This page was made by collecting and cross-checking the following kinds of information.

- **Papers and research databases**: a catalog of fish scientific names (［1］), a paper on geographic variation in *Moenkhausia bonita* (［2］), and FishBase (［3］)
- **Articles and guides from Japan**: an article (［4］), an online shop's list of all species it has handled in the past (［5］), a shop's fish guide page (［6］), a tropical fish guide site (［7］), and an aquarium's blog (［8］)
- **Shop records from Japan**: shops' fish guide pages, blogs and arrival information (［9］〜［16］), and auction results (［17］)
- **Records of regulations**: Brazil's rules on ornamental fish (［18］), the CITES species database (［19］), and pages of the Ministry of the Environment, Japanese law and the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (［20］〜［24］)

There are 4 rules for reading this page (the meanings of terms are also collected in the [glossary](/aqua-species-note/en/glossary/)).

1. **Numbers have sources.** A ［number］ corresponds to the reference list at the end; clicking it takes you to that reference.
2. **"(needs checking)"** marks things such as hearsay or short shop descriptions, which may have been misheard or miswritten.
3. **"(unverified)"** marks things whose original source could not be opened and which could not be confirmed.
4. **"Data from a related species"** means numbers for fish other than this one (such as *Moenkhausia bonita*). Whether this fish is even close to *M. bonita* is not yet known either. The author's interpretations are marked "(inference)".

And **what is not known is written down as not known.** The scientific name of this fish could not be found. No record measuring the water of the habitat, no record of the water temperature or chemistry used for keeping, and no breeding record have been found. The dated records from Japan are concentrated in spring 2019, and no later record of arrivals was found in the searches made this time. The open questions are collected in "7. ## 2. Identity {#identity}

### Conclusion — a trade name with no known scientific name, given to separate fish by origin

To give the conclusion first: **"New black cherry tetra" is a trade name that Japanese shops gave to a small characin imported in spring 2019 from the (upper) Tocantins River, Brazil.** It was sold as a fish of a different origin from the "Black cherry" that had earlier come in from Mato Grosso (［4］［9］［10］［11］［13］).

**Not a single source giving a scientific name for this fish was found this time.** Whether it is a described species, a species that has no name yet (sp.), or a population of the same species as the original "Black cherry" from a different origin, is not known.

|---|---|---|
| Japanese shop's blog (March 7, 2019) | 「ニューブラックチェリーテトラ トカンチンス上流」("New black cherry tetra, upper Tocantins"). A fish of a different origin from the Black cherry that had arrived before; slimmer than the Mato Grosso fish | ［10］ |
| Japanese shop's blog (March 8, 2019) | 「ニューブラックチェリーテトラ」("New black cherry tetra") (Tocantins). Much more slender than the Mato Grosso fish | ［13］ |
| Japanese shop's blog (March 14, 2019) | 「ニューブラックチェリーテトラ アルトトカンチンス産」("New black cherry tetra, from Alto Tocantins"). A fish of a different origin from the Black cherry tetra that had arrived before | ［11］ |
| Japanese shop's online store page (creation date shown as March 2019) | Lists 2 "New black cherry tetras" side by side, one "from Alto Tocantins" and one "upper Tocantins" | ［12］ |

None of the sources give a scientific name. The "New" in the name is used not to mean a new species, but to show a difference in origin (［4］［9］［10］［11］).

### The original "Black cherry"

The "Black cherry" that the "New" is based on is also a fish that is rarely given a scientific name. The dated records from Japan are as follows.

| Period | Name and origin | Source |
|---|---|---|
| November 5, 2012 | 「ブラックチェリーヘミグラムステトラ」(Black cherry hemigrammus tetra) (3–3.5 cm) | ［14］ |
| December 2, 2012 | 「ヘミグラムス sp. "ミナスブラックチェリー"」(Hemigrammus sp. "Minas black cherry") (described as "from the south") | ［16］ |
| April 6, 2018 | 「ブラックチェリーヘミグラムス」(Black cherry hemigrammus) (「マッドグロッソ」, "Mad Grosso") | ［14］［15］ |
| March 2019 | The Black cherry "that had arrived before" (from Mato Grosso) | ［10］［13］ |
{caption="Records from Japan of the original Black cherry"}

- "Minas" (「ミナス」) is probably the Brazilian state of Minas Gerais, and「マッドグロッソ」and「マットグロッソ」(Mad Grosso, Mato Grosso) the state of Mato Grosso (inference). This means that the same name "Black cherry" was used together with the names of 2 states. Whether they are the same fish is not known.
- The names include "hemigrammus" (the genus *Hemigrammus*) (［14］［15］［16］), but no source confirming that it belongs to that genus was found.
- An online shop's list of all species it has handled in the past gives the scientific name *Moenkhausia bonita* for「ブラックチェリー・ヘミグラムス」(Black cherry hemigrammus), its range as the Brazilian states of Mato Grosso and Minas Gerais, and its other name as「モンクホーシャ・ボニータ」(Moenkhausia bonita) (［5］). Among the sources found this time, this is the only one that gives a scientific name for the "Black cherry". It does not say what this is based on.

### What kind of fish is *Moenkhausia bonita*?

*Moenkhausia bonita*, the name given for the original "Black cherry", is the following kind of fish. **What is written here is about *M. bonita*, not about the New black cherry (data from a related species).**

| Item | Details | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Description | Described by Benine, Castro & Sabino in 2004. Still a valid name | ［1］ |
| Type locality | The Baía Bonita River, in the upper Miranda River of the Paraguay River system, Mato Grosso do Sul, Brazil | ［1］ |
| Distribution | The Paraguay and upper Paraná river systems (Paraguay, Uruguay, Argentina, Brazil). A 2019 paper says it also occurs in the Madeira River system | ［1］［2］ |
| How to identify it | The rear edges of both the upper and lower lobes of the tail fin are black, and there is a diamond-shaped black spot at the base of the tail fin | ［3］ |
| Colors in life | Some fish have orange or reddish anal, tail and dorsal fins, and some pale yellow ones (this may be a difference between the sexes, but it has not been confirmed) | ［2］ |
| Size | One statement gives a maximum of 44 mm standard length (body length excluding the tail fin), and there is a record of a specimen of 49.4 mm among those examined | ［2］ |
| Trade names in Japan | 「モンクホーシャ・ボニータ」(Moenkhausia bonita),「マジックテールテトラ」(Magic tail tetra) | ［6］ |
{caption="Records of Moenkhausia bonita (data from a related species)"}

- The Tocantins River is not included in the distribution in the catalog (［1］). The 2019 paper says that this species occurs in the Río de la Plata system and in the Madeira River system (a tributary of the Amazon), and regards 1 lot of specimens taken in the Guaporé River, in the upper Madeira, as this species, but those specimens were not examined in that paper (［2］). FishBase still writes that it is "known only from the type locality" (［3］), which is older than the catalog and the paper.
- Both the upper Paraguay River and the Guaporé River flow through the state of Mato Grosso. Even if the "Black cherry from Mato Grosso" is *M. bonita*, this does not conflict with the distribution (inference). However, no record from the Tocantins River was found, either in the catalog or in the paper (［1］［2］).
- The 2019 paper writes that in fish of the genus *Hemigrammus* the lateral line (the row of scales along the side of the body with sensory pores) runs only part of the way, while in *M. bonita* it continues to the tail (［2］). Even if it is sold under the name "hemigrammus", it is safer to think that a shop's name is not a judgment of classification (inference).
- A Japanese aquarium's blog (October 2019) introduces *M. bonita*, seen by a staff member in the Pantanal in Brazil, as a fish with red dorsal and anal fins (［8］).

### As a fish of the Tocantins River

The catalog of fish scientific names lists at least *M. alesis*, *M. aurantia*, *M. dasalmas*, *M. goya*, *M. hysterosticta*, *M. pankilopteryx* and *M. tergimacula* as species of the genus *Moenkhausia* described from the Tocantins River system (including the Araguaia River) (［1］). Whether the New black cherry is one of these, a species that has no name yet, a not-yet-known population of *M. bonita*, or a fish of a different genus altogether **cannot be decided from the sources this time**. No source comparing the features of these species with the New black cherry was found either.

### A different fish with a similar name

Among the small characins with "cherry" in their name, there is also the「レッドチェリーテトラ」(Red cherry tetra). It is introduced under the name *Hyphessobrycon* sp. 'Red Cherry', and is a different fish whose whole body turns orange (［7］). Searching by name alone may lead you to a different fish (inference).

### How the "New" differs from the earlier fish (shops' observations)

Shops' blogs and fish guide pages describe the following differences and features. All of them are short shop descriptions, and **need checking**.

- Slimmer than the Mato Grosso fish, with a different look (［10］). A much more slender body (［13］). A slim body with a large tail fin (［9］).
- Red on the tail fin. The article writes "red spots within the large tail fin" (translated) (［4］), and a shop writes "the tail fin is tinged with red" (translated) (［9］).
- A large silvery, shining part (silver tip) on the tips of the fins (［4］［9］).
- The colors come out quickly, and it spreads its fins wide to display (［11］).

A shop wonders whether the body is sharper because the fish lives upstream (［10］), but this is the shop's guess.

## 3. Habitat {#habitat}

### Distribution — shop labels only

All that is known about where this fish comes from is the shop labels "Tocantins" (［9］), "upper Tocantins" (［10］) and "Alto Tocantins" (［11］［12］). "Alto" means "upper" in Portuguese, so all of them probably refer to the upper Tocantins River (inference). No source saying in which state, or in which tributary, it was collected was found.

### Water temperature and chemistry — no measurements found

No record measuring the water temperature, pH or electrical conductivity of the habitat was found.

For reference, FishBase writes that *M. bonita*, the name given for the original "Black cherry", has been collected at the edges of slow-flowing rivers (about 0.1 m per second), in places up to 1 m deep, swims in groups of 10–30 near the surface, and mainly eats insects falling from the surrounding forest (［3］; **data from a related species**). Whether the New black cherry lives in a similar environment is not known.

## 4. Breeding records compared {#breeding}

**Not a single record of this fish breeding in captivity was found, either in Japan or abroad.** No record of a failed attempt to get them to spawn has been found either.

- Searches were made in Japanese and English for「ニューブラックチェリー」(New black cherry),「ブラックチェリーテトラ」(Black cherry tetra),「ブラックチェリー ヘミグラムス」(Black cherry hemigrammus) and the name *Moenkhausia bonita*. Only shops' arrival records and short introductions in articles were found.

- Abroad, no source introducing this fish under the name "black cherry" was found at all.

### In the species given for the original "Black cherry"

There is a record of *M. bonita* being sold at a Japanese shop labeled「ブリード」(bred; fish bred in an aquarium) (stock information updated January 17, 2021; ［6］; **data from a related species**). However, it does not say who bred them or how. Whether this applies as it is to the New black cherry is not known either.

## 5. Keeping and breeding {#care}

### What is known

There are very few records of how to keep it.

- It eats prepared food well, so it is easy to keep (［4］). There is no particular trouble with food (［9］).
- It is small, about 3 cm long, peaceful in temperament, and suited to a community tank (［4］). It is not aggressive (［9］).

Both are short descriptions by shops or articles, and they do not say how many fish were kept, in what water, or for how long (**needs checking**).

### What is not known

- No source giving guidelines for the water temperature, pH or hardness for keeping was found.
- No record of lifespan was found either.
- How to tell the sexes apart is not known either. In *M. bonita*, some fish have orange or red fins and some pale yellow ones, and this is thought to be a difference between the sexes, but it has not been confirmed (［2］; **data from a related species**).

### Breeding

Since there are no breeding records, this page cannot describe how to breed them (chapter 4).

### Legality and regulation {#legal}

Because the scientific name of this fish is not known, **it is not possible to check, at the level of the species, which rules of which countries apply.** Characins are not listed by CITES (the Washington Convention), and, as far as was checked this time, they are not among the species whose import or keeping is regulated by Japanese law either. However, **it is not known where, or under which permits, the fish in the trade were collected and exported, so this page cannot state whether the trade in wild fish was legal.** What is known and what is not known are written separately below.

#### What is known

- **CITES**: searching the species database of the CITES Secretariat and others (Species+) for *Moenkhausia* (genus), *Hemigrammus* (genus), Characidae (family), Acestrorhamphidae (family) and Characiformes (order) gave no records (checked November 18, 2026) (［19］). FishBase also shows "Not Evaluated" for *M. bonita* (［3］).
- **Which country they come from**: the shop labels give the (upper) Tocantins River, Brazil (［9］〜［12］).
- **Brazil's rules on ornamental fish**: in Brazil, a 2012 ordinance (Instrução Normativa Interministerial MPA/MMA nº 1, de 3 de janeiro de 2012) lists in its Annex I the native freshwater fish that may be caught as ornamental fish (the items are numbered up to 725). Native species that are not in Annex I may not be caught or sold as ornamental fish, unless there is a special rule (［18］). For species that have no name yet, only those listed in Annex I together with the number of a reference specimen are allowed (［18］).
- **Contents of Annex I**: the 2012 Annex I lists 20 species of the genus *Moenkhausia*, such as *M. intermedia* and *M. dichroura*, but *M. bonita* was not among them (［18］). No entry for an unnamed species (sp.) of the genera *Moenkhausia* or *Hemigrammus* was found either (［18］).
- **IUCN Red List**: *M. bonita* is listed as "Least Concern (LC)" (［1］［3］). Since the species of the New black cherry is not known, whether it has been assessed is not known either.
- **An example from another region of Brazil**: a Japanese aquarium's blog (October 2019) writes that in the Pantanal, which the staff member visited, laws and rules did not allow fish to be caught or exported (［8］). This is not about the Tocantins River, and it does not say which rules this was based on.

#### What is not known

- Since the species of this fish is not known, whether it is a species listed in Brazil's Annex I cannot be checked (**unverified**).
- Whether the 2012 ordinance is still in use without amendment, or whether Annex I has been updated, has not been checked (**unverified**). An attempt was made to open the 2025 guide to the regulations of Brazil's Ministry of Fisheries and Aquaculture, but the PDF could not be obtained.
- Whether this fish is covered by Brazilian national or state (such as Tocantins or Goiás) lists of threatened species, or by state rules, has not been checked (**unverified**).
- From which exporter, and with what documents, the fish that came to Japan in 2019 were imported is not known, because it is not labeled (［9］〜［11］).
- So it is not known whether the wild fish in the trade were collected and exported legally.

#### Japanese regulations: date checked

Checked on 2026-11-18. However, because the scientific name is not known, what was checked was "whether characins (the order Characiformes) are covered".

- **Invasive Alien Species Act**: the Ministry of the Environment's "List of Invasive Alien Species, etc." and the list of regulated organisms (PDF) were checked. The only fish on them are some members of the orders Lepisosteiformes (gars), Cypriniformes, Siluriformes, Esociformes (pikes), Cyprinodontiformes and Perciformes; Characiformes were not listed as an Invasive Alien Species, an Uncategorized Alien Species, or an organism that requires a species identification certificate (［20］).
- **List of Alien Species That May Damage Ecosystems** (not a legal regulation, but a list to call for caution): no characins (including *Moenkhausia* and *Hemigrammus*) were in the file listing fish (［21］).
- **Act on Welfare and Management of Animals (Specified Animals)**: on the Ministry of the Environment's page, the Specified Animals are about 650 species of mammals, birds and reptiles, such as tigers, bears, crocodiles and mamushi (a pit viper) (［22］). The list of Specified Animals also covers only the classes Mammalia, Aves and Reptilia, and **fish are not covered** (［22］).
- **Act on Conservation of Endangered Species**: in the text of the enforcement order (e-Gov law search), neither the names *Moenkhausia* and *Hemigrammus* nor the name of any characin was found (［23］).
- **Fisheries Resources Protection Act (import permits)**: on the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries' page, the fish that need a permit to import are the salmon family (Salmonidae), common carp, the genus *Carassius* including goldfish, bighead carp, silver carp, black carp, grass carp, Nile tilapia and red sea bream (the list in force from June 9, 2026), and no characins were included (［24］).
- **Washington Convention (CITES)**: as above, characins are not listed (［19］).
- Whether this fish is covered by **other rules** (such as prefectural ordinances) has not been checked yet (**unverified**).

#### This page's position

> **This page does not state whether the wild fish in the trade were legally collected and exported.** As above, it sets out what is known and what is not known separately. Not being regulated under Japanese law does not mean that collecting and export in the country of origin were legal. In particular, because the scientific name of this fish is not known, it has not even been possible to check whether it is on Brazil's list of species that may be caught as ornamental fish.

## 7. What is still unknown {#unknowns}

Below are the gaps that the sources collected this time could not fill, and the points marked "(unverified)" or "(needs checking)" in the text.

1. **Scientific name**: no source giving a scientific name for the "New black cherry" has been found. Whether it is a described species, a species with no name, or the same species as the original "Black cherry", is not known.
2. **Identity of the original "Black cherry"**: only 1 source gives *Moenkhausia bonita* (［5］), and it gives no basis. The name "Black cherry" has been used together with 2 places of origin, Mato Grosso and Minas Gerais (［10］［14］［15］［16］), and whether they are the same fish is not known either.
3. **Conflict in the distribution**: the range of *M. bonita* is given as the upper Paraguay and Paraná rivers and the Madeira River system (［1］［2］), and no record from the Tocantins River has been found. This does not match the shops' origin labels (［9］〜［12］).
4. **How to identify it**: the features shops describe (slim body, red in the tail fin, silvery parts at the tips of the fins) are short descriptions and **need checking**. No source compares them with the features in the papers.
5. **Water of the habitat**: no measurements of water temperature, pH or electrical conductivity have been found. The tributary and state where it was collected are not known either.
6. **Keeping**: no records of guidelines for water temperature and chemistry, of lifespan, or of how to tell the sexes apart have been found. The descriptions of how to keep it (［4］［9］) **need checking**.
7. **Breeding**: no record of breeding in captivity, and no record of failure, has been found.
8. **Trade**: the only dated records are from March to May 2019. Whether there were later arrivals, and whether any fish are being kept in Japan now, is not known.
9. **Price**: there are only 3 records, all from 2019, and they do not say whether tax is included.
10. **Rules in the country of origin**: whether it is a species covered by Brazil's Annex I, whether the 2012 ordinance is still in use in the same form, and how it is treated for protection nationally and by the states, are **unverified**.
11. **Japanese regulations**: because the scientific name is not known, only the range of characins was checked. Prefectural ordinances are **unverified**.
12. **Sources that could not be opened**: the original description of *M. bonita* (2004, Copeia; bibliographic details checked in［1］), the 2025 guide to the regulations of Brazil's Ministry of Fisheries and Aquaculture (the PDF could not be obtained), and the CITES appendices page (it could not be opened because an automated check screen appeared, so the check was made by searching Species+).

If you have kept the New black cherry tetra and record **the origin label, when you bought it, its body features (whether the lateral line continues to the tail, the black spot at the base of the tail fin, the color of the tail fin), the water temperature and chemistry, how many years it lived, and whether you saw spawning**, that will be very useful as a record that can be compared with others. In particular, there are still almost no clues for deciding what this fish is.
